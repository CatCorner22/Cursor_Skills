## 1. Informed consent under (and about) altered states

**Why distinctive.**
- Consent is given in a sober state for events that will occur while the person's capacity, perception, and suggestibility are altered.
- The *content* of the risk is unusual: lasting change in beliefs, values, or sense of self.
  - Research reports that psychedelic experiences can shift metaphysical beliefs (e.g., Timmermann et al., *Sci Rep* 2021 [M]).
  - **Smith & Sisti, "Ethics and ego dissolution: the case of psilocybin," *J Med Ethics* 2021;47:807–814** [V]. They argue that psilocybin's personality- and worldview-changing effects create novel risks that warrant an *enhanced* consent process, and they supply discussion prompts.
  - **Caporuscio, "Transient or transformative? Psychedelics, agency and informed consent," *J Med Ethics* 2026, doi 10.1136/jme-2025-111248** [V]. The counter-argument: lasting change usually requires a sober, agential process *after* the session, so the "transformative experience" objection to pre-session consent is weaker than claimed.
  - Both draw on L.A. Paul's *Transformative Experience* (2014) [M]. Present both positions.

**Competing positions.**
- *Enhanced consent:*
  - detailed disclosure of belief-change and identity risks;
  - staged consent (preparation, dosing, integration);
  - a pre-agreed session plan;
  - consent to touch only before dosing, plus an in-session right to stop.
- *Proportionality:*
  - over-disclosure of speculative risks may raise anxiety and worsen outcomes (a nocebo effect);
  - consent should match ordinary drug consent plus specific session elements.

**Safeguards in use.**
- Oregon's state-written consent form with itemized acknowledgments [V].
- MAPS Code sections on safety, transparency, and touch [V].
- FDA expectations for direct observation and discharge-readiness criteria [V/S].

**Reviewer questions.**
1. What exactly was disclosed about belief change, about difficult experiences, and about the uncertainty of durability?
2. Could participants withdraw consent to touch, music, or presence *during* the session? How was that operationalized?
3. Did recruitment materials or media exposure create therapeutic misconception?
4. Was capacity assessed before dosing? Was a re-consent step built into redosing decisions?

## 2. Touch, boundaries, and sexual misconduct

**Why distinctive.** Long sessions (4–12+ hours), intense emotions, regression, transference, and suggestibility. The client often has a trauma history. Many models use "supportive" or "therapeutic" touch, which mainstream psychotherapy generally avoids.

**Competing positions.**
- *Touch can be therapeutic and reparative* when consented to and bounded. The MAPS model historically allowed nurturing touch [S].
- *No-touch or minimal-touch rules,* because a client in an altered state cannot reliably consent in the moment, and the slippery-slope risk is high (Gutheil & Gabbard's crossing-to-violation pathway [M]).

**Existing rules.**
- **Oregon:** touch only when requested by the client, with prior written consent; limited to hugs or hands on hands, feet, or shoulders [V].
- **Colorado:** 10 required training hours on relational boundaries and touch [S].
- **MAPS Code:** §5 Touch and §6 Sexual Boundaries [V: titles].

**Reviewer questions.**
1. What touch was permitted, who could initiate it, and how was consent documented?
2. Were sessions recorded, and was there independent review of the recordings?
3. Were two facilitators present?
4. Was there a reporting channel independent of the therapists and the sponsor?

## 3. Case study: MAPS MP4 Canadian site (Buisson)

**Facts as reported** [V: MAPS statements (maps.org/?p=10926; 2022-03-01); S: CBC; Quartz; Psymposia]:
- **Who and when.** Meaghan Buisson was a participant in a MAPS-sponsored Phase 2 MDMA-for-PTSD study (MP4) in Vancouver. Her sessions were in 2015, and the trial was terminated in 2016. Therapists: Richard Yensen and Donna Dryer.
- **What the videos show.** Session footage from 2015 was released in March 2022 through New York magazine's *Cover Story: Power Trip* podcast. It shows the therapists cuddling, "spooning," and pinning down the participant. MAPS staff say they first viewed the footage in November 2021.
- **The lawsuit.**
  - In 2018 Buisson sued Yensen and Dryer in the Supreme Court of British Columbia, **alleging sexual assault**.
  - Yensen admits a post-trial sexual relationship but says she initiated it. MAPS dates the relationship to late 2015 / early 2016.
  - The case **settled out of court**.
- **MAPS response:**
  - after her October 2018 complaint, reported the matter to the FDA, Health Canada, and the ethics committee;
  - publicly called it an "ethical violation" and cut ties with the therapists;
  - paid **C$15,000** (Canadian dollars) toward her therapy "on a compassionate basis" while the case was pending. MAPS's counsel said the legal responsibility lay with the therapists;
  - formalized its Code of Ethics (2019; revised 2021).
- **Criticism.** Critics say MAPS did not tighten who may act as a therapist, and that the videos were available for review during the trial [S].
- **Later events.** On 2024-08-10, *Psychopharmacology* retracted three papers tied to MAPS/Lykos MDMA data. The notice cites protocol violations amounting to unethical conduct at the MP4 study site [V: Springer notice]. The FDA advisory committee in 2024 heard concerns about misconduct reporting [S].

**Lessons a review can draw** (label as ANALYTICAL JUDGMENT):
1. Codes adopted *after* a violation reveal the governance gap that existed before it.
2. Video recording deters violations only if someone independent reviews the recordings.
3. Boundary violations are adverse events and data-integrity events. Their non-reporting distorts both safety and efficacy evidence.

Use neutral language: "alleged," "reported," "according to court filings." Do not add details beyond the sources.

## 4. Vulnerability, suggestibility, and influence on beliefs

- **Heightened suggestibility** under psychedelics is reported in experimental work [M]. It creates risks of:
  - facilitator influence on metaphysical or spiritual beliefs;
  - false or distorted memories during trauma work (cf. the hypnosis and recovered-memory controversies [M]);
  - financial or community exploitation, especially in retreat settings.
- **Competing positions:**
  - *Belief change is part of how it works,* so blocking it may block benefit.
  - *Neutrality is owed.* Facilitators must not steer content; the client's interpretations belong to the client (chaplaincy no-proselytizing norms; APA respect for client values).
- **Reviewer questions:**
  1. Do manuals tell facilitators to avoid interpreting content?
  2. Are spiritual frameworks presented as optional?
  3. Is there a cooling-off period before major life decisions or donations?

## 5. Therapist role, credentials, and the drug-plus-therapy model

- **Regulatory gap.** FDA approves the drug and can require REMS elements (certified settings, monitoring). It does not license or regulate psychotherapy, which is a state matter [S: CBS on the FDA's limits].
- **Model divergence:**
  - drug plus manualized therapy (MDMA-assisted therapy);
  - drug plus "psychological support" (COMP360; Usona's facilitators);
  - drug alone with monitoring (DT120; TSND-201; 5-MeO-DMT programs).
- **Ethical stakes by model:**

  | Model | Ethical stakes |
  |---|---|
  | Therapy-heavy | Workforce, cost, access, unregulated "facilitator" credentials, and boundary risk |
  | Monitoring-only | Less support during difficult experiences; integration is left to the patient |

- **Reviewer questions:**
  1. Who is in the room, and with what license and training?
  2. Who supervises them, and what is the ratio of staff to patients?
  3. What is the plan for a difficult experience, for medical emergencies, and for after-care?

## 6. Research integrity: unblinding, expectancy, AE capture, retractions

See study-design-flaws.md §3 for the methods. The ethics angle:
- **Trials that cannot be blinded** may still be ethical and informative. But *claims* must match the design, and over-claiming is itself an ethical failure (spin).
- **Under-reporting adverse events** harms future patients. It is a specific lesson of the Lykos CRL, which said "positive" abuse-relevant effects went unreported, and that safety events at two sites were missed [S].
- **Advocacy-researcher overlap.** Many investigators are also advocates. That is an intellectual conflict of interest that needs disclosure and independent replication.

## 7. Hype, advocacy, and the speed-vs-rigor debate (2026)

**Context (facts)** [V/S: fda-pipeline.md §2]:
- EO 14401 (April 2026) declares acceleration a national priority.
- Commissioner's National Priority Vouchers target 1–2-month reviews for psilocybin and methylone.
- Final FDA guidance (July 2026); FDA public hearing (September 2026).
- High-profile advocacy, including by public figures at the signing [S].

**Program-specific facts that critics and defenders both cite** [V/S; details and sources in fda-pipeline.md §2a]:
- **No statute or regulation.** The CNPV program has no statutory or regulatory basis, and its legal authority is disputed (Rep. Auchincloss; AP reporting).
- **Approvals unsigned.** Two senior officials reportedly declined to sign approvals under it.
- **Compass's voucher.** COMP360 was dropped from the October 2025 list by HHS and White House officials, then given a voucher six days after EO 14401 (STAT, anonymous sources).
- **Leadership.** The commissioner who created the program resigned in May 2026. HHS says the program continues.
- **Record.** About 10 approvals and 1 rejection as of August 2026.
- **The defense.** For Compass, FDA has reviewed modules on a rolling basis since April 2026, so the "1–2 months" covers only the final review step.

**Separate three questions** (the merits differ):
1. Was *this drug's* evidence adequate? An evidence question.
2. Is *this review process* adequate for a first-in-class drug with unblinding problems? A process question: advisory committee, published review, REMS design.
3. Is the *program* lawful and legitimate? A legal and institutional question.

An approval can be scientifically sound and procedurally contestable at the same time.

**Arguments for acceleration** (as its proponents make them):
- large unmet need (TRD, PTSD, veteran suicide);
- multiple positive Phase 3 trials;
- decades of Schedule I barriers to research;
- breakthrough designations already granted on preliminary evidence;
- patients face harm from delay.

**Arguments for caution** (as critics make them):
- functional unblinding and expectancy may inflate effects;
- durability evidence is largely open-label or responder-conditioned;
- 1–2-month reviews leave less time to scrutinize novel REMS, abuse liability, and site-level misconduct;
- political pressure on an independent scientific agency sets a precedent;
- the 2024 MDMA review shows that serious problems surface only under full review.

**How the skill handles it.**
- Present both sides with sources.
- Separate the *evidence questions* (what the trials show) from the *value questions* (how much uncertainty to accept, for whom).
- Do not attribute motives to officials. Label any synthesis as ANALYTICAL JUDGMENT.

## 8. Access, cost, and justice

**Cost structure.** Drug cost, plus session time (multi-hour monitoring), plus therapist time, plus REMS site certification. Compass cites an existing network of about 8,000–8,500 interventional-psychiatry centers [S]. Access may track existing esketamine infrastructure, which is concentrated in some areas.

**Justice questions.**
1. Who gets access first?
2. Do insurers cover it?
3. What about rural patients, people without paid leave for all-day sessions, and people with criminal records from past drug enforcement?
4. Do nonprofit sponsors (Usona) change access?

**Representation in trials.** Historically, trial participants have been mostly white and highly educated [M]. Helsinki 2024 calls for responsible inclusion [V]. Check the demographic tables.

## 9. Indigenous knowledge, reciprocity, and conservation

- **Practices.** Mazatec use of psilocybin mushrooms (María Sabina; R. Gordon Wasson's 1957 *Life* article and its consequences [M]); Native American Church use of peyote; Amazonian ayahuasca traditions; Bwiti use of iboga; the Sonoran Desert toad.
- **Issues:**
  - attribution and acknowledgment;
  - benefit-sharing;
  - conservation (peyote; iboga; toad);
  - patents on synthetic formulations vs traditional use;
  - "spiritual extraction."
- **Positions:**
  - *Moral reciprocity is owed* regardless of whether the molecule is synthetic.
  - *Synthetic pharmaceuticals owe no legal duty,* and voluntary reciprocity is philanthropy.
  - Present both; note that the Nagoya Protocol does not bind the United States [M].

## 10. Commercialization, patents, and conflicts of interest

- **Patents.**
  - Polymorph and formulation patents on public-domain molecules: Compass cites a polymorph patent to 2038 [S]. Deuterated analogs (HLP003, HLP004) and salts or devices (BPL-003, GH001) are similar strategies.
  - Critics see "evergreening" of natural substances. Defenders cite the incentive to fund costly Phase 3 trials.
- **Investor-driven disclosure.** Topline data arrive by press release timed to markets.
- **Public benefit corporations and nonprofits.** Mission claims vs governance reality. Check board composition, profit distribution, and pricing commitments.

## 11. Non-medical (state) models vs the medical model

- **Oregon** frames psilocybin services as *not* medical treatment. No diagnosis is required, and facilitators may not make therapeutic claims [V/S].
- **Colorado** licenses facilitators and healing centers [V].
- **Ethical tension:** autonomy and access vs medical screening; harm-reduction vs consumer-protection models; equity of access when prices are high; unlicensed facilitators working with people who in fact have serious mental illness.

## 12. Special populations

- **Veterans and military.** Strong advocacy, VA research, and coercion risks if access is tied to benefits or duty status.
- **End-of-life patients.** Existential distress trials. Consent under time pressure; family involvement.
- **Adolescents.** Developmental risk; parental permission plus the minor's assent (45 CFR 46 Subpart D [M]). Little or no trial data.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Unknown risks; Oregon discloses this [V].
- **People with psychosis or bipolar risk.** Excluded from trials. Real-world screening accuracy is uncertain.
- **People in criminal-legal systems.** Prisoners are covered by Subpart C [M]. Court-ordered treatment raises coercion concerns.

## 13. Data, recording, and privacy

- **Sensitivity.** Session recordings contain highly sensitive disclosures. Ask who owns them, who may access them, how long they are retained, and whether they may be used for training.
- **Legal exposure.** Use of a Schedule I drug creates legal risk if records are subpoenaed. Certificates of Confidentiality for NIH-funded and other qualifying research protect against compelled disclosure [M].
- **Real-world data.** Post-approval REMS registries and digital monitoring raise consent and secondary-use questions. Helsinki 2024 addresses broad consent for data reuse [V].
