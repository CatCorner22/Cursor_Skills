# What a psychedelic code of ethics must address for optimal patient protection

**Last verified: 2026-11-09.** This file answers: "What must a code of ethics for psychedelic care or research contain to protect patients as fully as possible?" For each domain it gives the risk, a **minimum** standard (what the better existing rules already require), an **optimal** standard (the strongest protection that is still practical), and the **donor** sources. Donor details and tags live in ethics-codes-to-borrow.md, consent-review-checklist.md, international-access.md, and psychedelic-ethics-issues.md. When you draft actual code language, hand off to the `one-way-optimal-language` skill (active voice in every clause).

## 1. Why codes fail, and the design rules that follow

| Failure | Example | Design rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Aspiration without obligation** | "Practitioners should respect boundaries" | Write obligations as rules with a named actor and a verb of obligation; keep aspirations in a separate preamble |
| **No enforcement** | A voluntary code with no complaint route or sanction | Tie the code to licensing, employment, or contract; name the body that hears complaints and the sanctions it can impose |
| **Self-regulation captured by insiders** | Practitioners judging peers they train with | Independent, mixed panels that include public and former-client members |
| **Rules that depend on the vulnerable party** | Consent to touch given mid-session | Move decisions to a time when the client has capacity; let the client narrow consent at any time, never expand it |
| **Nobody watching** | MP4 video existed but no one reviewed it | Independent review of recordings; a second person present; routine audits |
| **Silence about money** | Upselling "integration packages" | Price transparency; ban on referral fees and inducements |
| **One-size rules** | The same rule for a medical trial and a wellness retreat | State the setting the code covers; add rules for higher-risk settings |
| **Codes the client never sees** | A professional code in a members-only document | A plain-language **client bill of rights** handed over and signed before the first session |

## 2. The 20 domains

| # | Domain | Risk | Minimum (best existing rules) | Optimal | Donors |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Scope and setting** | Rules written for trials applied to retreats, or the reverse | State which settings and roles the code covers | Tiered rules by setting (trial, clinic, licensed service center, ceremony) with stricter rules for higher risk | Oregon (non-medical) vs. TGA (medical); Colorado |
| 2 | **Competence and training** | Untrained or overconfident facilitators | Approved training hours, including ethics and touch; scope-of-practice limits | Competency-based certification with supervised practice, continuing education, and a ban on practising outside one's license | Colorado (150 hours, 25 in ethics, 10 in touch) [S]; TGA 2026 (trial experience or AP supervision); JAMA consensus pt 16 |
| 3 | **Personal use and impairment** | Facilitators under the influence, or advocacy distorting judgment | No use of substances during or before sessions; no requirement of personal use for licensure | Fitness-for-duty rules, plus disclosure of conflicts arising from advocacy roles | JAMA consensus pt 14; APA impairment standards [M] |
| 4 | **Screening and contraindications** | Psychosis, bipolar disorder, cardiac disease, lithium, MAOIs | Documented screening with exclusion criteria; medication review | Physician-level medical and psychiatric screening for higher-risk substances (ibogaine: ECG and cardiac monitoring); a documented reason for any exception | Oregon screening forms, including the lithium hard stop (OAR 333-333-5050(3)(a), (5)(a)) [V]; FDA guidance; psychedelic-history-and-substances.md §6–7 |
| 5 | **Informed consent: content** | Clients don't understand risks, the chance of no benefit, or the limits of evidence | 21 CFR 50.25 elements (research); state consent forms | Tell clients the full range of experiences, including **no response**; give absolute numbers for benefit and harm; disclose the evidence quality and funding sources | JAMA consensus pt 7; consent-review-checklist.md |
| 6 | **Informed consent: timing and capacity** | Consent under altered states, or consent "for the self you will become" (Smith & Sisti) | Consent before dosing; a right to withdraw at any time | Staged consent (before, during, after); decisions made while sober; in-session answers can only narrow consent; consent revisited at integration | Colorado Rule 6.6(H)(6); MAPS §5; Smith & Sisti 2021 |
| 7 | **Touch** | Boundary violation and abuse | A written touch contract before dosing; hands, feet, and shoulders only; client-initiated; a stop signal | Safety touch disclosed and defined separately; supportive touch can be refused at any time, even with impaired capacity; touch logged and video-reviewed | Colorado Rule 6.6; Oregon 333-333-5120(7); JAMA consensus pts 9–10; MAPS §5 |
| 8 | **Sexual and romantic conduct** | Exploitation during and after care | A ban during care; a post-care period (Oregon 1 year; Colorado 2 years) | A lifetime ban for clients seen in an altered state; a ban covering partners and family; mandatory reporting by colleagues | MAPS §6 (lifelong); Colorado Rule 6.10(B) |
| 9 | **Staffing and observation** | One person, closed door, 6 or more hours | A second person available; a medical emergency plan | Two trained people present for high-dose sessions (one registered clinician); prescriber present for administration (TGA); a client right to a chaperone of a chosen gender | Johnson, Richards & Griffiths 2008; TGA May 2026; AMA 1.2.4 |
| 10 | **Recording and privacy** | No record of misconduct, or recordings misused | Disclosed recording with written consent; secure storage; no sharing without consent | Continuous recording that facilitators cannot pause, reviewed by someone independent of the treating team; client access; fixed retention; explicit consent for any secondary use | Oregon 333-333-4640; Colorado 6.5(H), 6.18(G)(2); JAMA consensus pt 11; MAPS §3 |
| 11 | **Confidentiality and its limits** | False promises of total confidentiality | State the exceptions: mandatory reporting, duty to warn, regulator inspection | A plain list of every limit; a Certificate of Confidentiality in US research; no disclosure of psychedelic use to employers or insurers without consent | 21 CFR 50.25(a)(5); 42 USC 241(d); Oregon 333-333-5040 |
| 12 | **Suggestibility, beliefs, and undue influence** | Facilitators shaping metaphysical beliefs, recruiting into groups, or exploiting transference | Ban on imposing spiritual or religious beliefs | A ban on recruiting clients into the facilitator's organizations, retreats, or belief communities; disclosure of the facilitator's worldview framing; client-led interpretation in integration | APA respect for client values [M]; chaplaincy codes [M]; psychedelic-ethics-issues.md §4 |
| 13 | **Adverse events and emergencies** | Harms unrecorded or unmanaged | A written emergency plan; AE reporting to the regulator | Systematic capture of every adverse event, including "positive" psychoactive effects and delayed harms; public aggregate reporting; an independent safety monitor | FDA 2026 guidance; Oregon AE reporting; TGA six-monthly reports |
| 14 | **Integration and aftercare** | Abandonment after a destabilizing experience | Follow-up contact | A defined integration plan, a crisis line, referral pathways, and no extra fee for essential follow-up | Colorado (no separate integration fee) [S]; ACA/APA termination standards [M] |
| 15 | **Money and conflicts of interest** | Upselling, inducements, conflicted research | Fee disclosure; conflict disclosure | Published prices; a ban on referral fees, commissions, and multi-session "packages" sold during a session; equity stakes disclosed to clients and IRBs | AMA COI opinions [M]; Colorado limits [S] |
| 16 | **Advertising and claims** | Cure claims, hype, cherry-picked numbers | No curative claims in non-medical programs; no claims that an unapproved drug is safe or effective | Any efficacy statement gives absolute numbers and cites peer-reviewed evidence; testimonials banned or clearly labelled | Oregon "dishonest conduct" rule [S]; 21 CFR 312.7(a) |
| 17 | **Justice, access, and respect** | Benefits only for the wealthy; Indigenous knowledge appropriated | Non-discrimination | Sliding-scale or subsidized access; benefit-sharing and credit for Indigenous knowledge; consultation with source communities; sustainability (peyote, the *Incilius* toad) | JAMA consensus pts 1–6, 17; Nagoya Protocol [M]; Chacruna-type community guidance [M] |
| 18 | **Special populations** | Veterans, trauma survivors, people with disabilities, older adults | Screening and accommodation | Trauma-informed protocols; accessible settings; heightened consent safeguards for those with impaired capacity or dependent relationships | Helsinki 2024 (vulnerable groups) [V]; ABA 1.14 analogy |
| 19 | **Group sessions** | Diluted attention; peer pressure; contagion of distress | Caps on group size; staffing ratios | Lower caps for first-time clients; a client right to leave the group space with support | Oregon group rules; Colorado group limits [S] |
| 20 | **Research participants** | Therapeutic misconception; data misuse; trial misconduct | IRB approval; GCP; registration | Independent DSMB; pre-registered protocols; open data; participant voices in oversight; harms research (McNamee, Devenot & Buisson 2023) | ICH E6(R3); Helsinki 2024; JAMA consensus pts 6, 8, 13, 15, 18 |

## 3. Enforcement architecture

A code protects patients only if five things are true:
1. **A complaint route the client knows about,** given in writing before the first session, with an independent recipient (not the facilitator's employer alone).
2. **Investigation by people without a stake,** including public and former-client members.
3. **Graduated sanctions** (warning, supervision, suspension, revocation) and **public disciplinary records**.
4. **Duty to report** colleague misconduct, with **whistleblower protection**.
5. **Coverage of unlicensed facilitators** through the license, contract, or employment that lets them practise. The MP4 case showed that a licensing board could not reach an unlicensed co-therapist (psychedelic-ethics-issues.md §3).

## 4. Model provisions (illustrative)

These show the shape of enforceable rules. Run final language through `one-way-optimal-language`; explain each drafting choice to Blake.
- **Touch.** "The facilitator must not touch the client except (a) as the client's signed touch agreement allows, and then only when the client asks during the session, or (b) to prevent imminent physical injury. The client may withdraw or narrow consent to touch at any time by word or by the agreed signal."
- **Recording.** "The service center must record every dosing session continuously on equipment the facilitator cannot pause. A reviewer who is not part of the treating team must review each recording within [14] days."
- **Sexual conduct.** "A facilitator must never engage in sexual or romantic conduct with a client, a client's partner, or a client's immediate family member, during care or at any time afterward."
- **Confidentiality limits.** "Before the first session, the facilitator must give the client a written list of every circumstance in which the facilitator will disclose information without the client's consent."
- **Claims.** "No one acting for the service may state or imply that a psychedelic cures, treats, or prevents a condition unless the statement gives absolute numbers and cites published, peer-reviewed evidence."

## 5. Auditing an existing code

1. List the 20 domains. Mark each as *absent*, *aspirational only*, *minimum*, or *optimal*.
2. For each provision, check four things: is there a named actor, an obligation, a trigger, and an enforcement route?
3. Check the timing of every consent provision against capacity: is consent obtained while sober?
4. Check who watches: second person, recording, independent review.
5. Check money and claims.
6. Check enforcement architecture (§3).
7. Report the gaps in a table with the donor provision for each. Put the absolute-vs-relative framing table beside any efficacy claim in the code's marketing.
