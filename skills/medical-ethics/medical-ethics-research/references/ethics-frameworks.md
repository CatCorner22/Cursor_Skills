# Ethics Frameworks: Medical, Research, Therapy, Psychiatric, and Bioethics

Use this file to pick the right lens and to name things correctly. A good ethics review:
1. names the frameworks it applies;
2. applies them to the specific facts;
3. shows where the frameworks conflict, and how it resolved the conflict;
4. marks which conclusions are contestable.

## 1. Normative theories (the "why")

| Theory | Core question | Strength | Typical blind spot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consequentialism / utilitarianism | Which option produces the best overall outcomes? | Takes aggregate welfare and evidence seriously | Can sacrifice individuals; depends on uncertain predictions |
| Deontology (Kantian) | What duties and rights constrain us, whatever the outcome? | Protects dignity, consent, truth-telling | Rigid when duties conflict |
| Virtue ethics | What would a person of good character (honest, compassionate, prudent) do? | Fits professional formation and therapeutic relationships | Underdetermines hard choices |
| Care ethics | What do relationships of dependency and vulnerability require? | Strong on power, trust, vulnerability (central in therapy) | Less guidance at the policy level |
| Contractualism / justice as fairness (Rawls) | What principles could no one reasonably reject? | Fair distribution, the least-advantaged | Abstract |
| Communitarian / relational autonomy | How do community, culture, and relationships shape choice? | Indigenous and collective interests | Can underweight the individual |
| Casuistry | How do similar past cases guide this one? | Practical, analogical | Depends on choosing the right paradigm cases |

## 2. Principlism (the workhorse)

Beauchamp & Childress, *Principles of Biomedical Ethics* (8th ed., 2019 [M]). There are four prima facie principles, specified and balanced against one another:
1. **Respect for autonomy.** Informed consent, truth-telling, confidentiality, and non-coercion.
2. **Nonmaleficence.** Do not inflict harm. It includes the duty of due care (negligence), and the doctrine of double effect in some traditions.
3. **Beneficence.** Promote welfare. It covers both positive beneficence and utility (weighing benefits against risks and costs). Paternalism arises when beneficence overrides autonomy.
4. **Justice.** Fair distribution of benefits, burdens, and access. Theories include utilitarian, libertarian, egalitarian, communitarian, capability, and well-being approaches.

Methods: **specification** (narrowing a principle to the context), **balancing** (weighing in a particular case), and **reflective equilibrium** (testing principles against considered judgments).

Critiques to acknowledge: the principles are "a checklist without a theory" (Clouser & Gert [M]); Western individualism; underweighting of care and relationships.

## 3. Case-based and practical methods

- **The four-box (four-topics) method** (Jonsen, Siegler & Winslade, *Clinical Ethics* [M]):
  1. medical indications;
  2. patient preferences;
  3. quality of life;
  4. contextual features (family, law, cost, institution, conflicts of interest).
- **Casuistry** (Jonsen & Toulmin, *The Abuse of Casuistry*, 1988 [M]): reason from paradigm cases.
- **Narrative ethics.** Attend to the patient's story and meaning. This is relevant to psychedelic experiences that change a person's values and beliefs.
- **Clinical ethics consultation standards** (ASBH *Core Competencies* [M]): facilitation, not decree.

## 4. Research ethics: history, documents, and rules

- **Nuremberg Code (1947).** Voluntary consent is "absolutely essential." There must be a favorable risk-benefit balance and the right to withdraw. It came from the Doctors' Trial.
- **Declaration of Helsinki** (World Medical Association; first adopted 1964).
  - **Current version: the 2024 revision, adopted 2024-10-19 in Helsinki** [V]. Its changes include:
    - "participants" replaces "subjects";
    - stronger justice and equity language, including responsible inclusion of underrepresented groups;
    - informed consent as an ongoing process, including electronic consent;
    - broad ("expanded") consent for secondary use of data and samples;
    - post-trial access obligations;
    - research integrity;
    - environmental sustainability [V: Cuatrecasas; Practical Neurology].
  - **Placebo controls** remain restricted where proven interventions exist, except under specified scientific necessity with no serious or irreversible harm (the long-standing Helsinki placebo paragraph [M]).
- **Belmont Report (1979)** [M]. Three principles, each with an application:

  | Principle | Application |
  |---|---|
  | Respect for persons (autonomy; protection of those with diminished autonomy) | Informed consent |
  | Beneficence | Assessment of risks and benefits |
  | Justice | Fair selection of subjects |

- **U.S. Common Rule (45 CFR 46)**, revised 2018 with general compliance from 2019 [M]. It covers IRB review, informed consent, exemptions, and single-IRB review for cooperative research. Its subparts:

  | Subpart | Population |
  |---|---|
  | A | The core rule |
  | B | Pregnant women, fetuses, neonates |
  | C | Prisoners |
  | D | Children |

- **FDA regulations** [M]: 21 CFR Part 50 (informed consent), Part 56 (IRBs), Part 312 (INDs), Part 314 (NDAs). FDA-regulated trials follow these whatever the funding source.
- **CIOMS International Ethical Guidelines for Health-related Research Involving Humans** (2016 [M]): research in low-resource settings, community engagement, and benefit-sharing.
- **ICH E6(R3) Good Clinical Practice** (adopted 2025-01-06; FDA final guidance 2025-09-09; Annex 2 on non-traditional trials endorsed 2026-06) [V]: risk-based quality management, critical-to-quality factors, and participant protection.
- **Clinical equipoise** (Freedman, *NEJM* 1987 [M]). A trial is ethical when honest professional disagreement exists about which arm is better. Distinguish it from individual "theoretical" equipoise.
- **Therapeutic misconception** (Appelbaum et al., 1982 [M]). Participants believe research procedures are designed for their individual benefit. It is a major risk in psychedelic trials, where media hype is heavy.
- **Therapeutic optimism vs misestimation.** Overestimating benefit probability is distinct from misunderstanding the purpose of research [M].
- **Placebo ethics.** Is a placebo arm justified when effective treatments exist? Add-on designs, active placebos, and rescue provisions are the usual answers.
- **Post-trial access** (Helsinki 2024 [V]). What happens to responders when the trial ends and the drug is unapproved and Schedule I?
- **Conflicts of interest.** Financial, intellectual, and allegiance conflicts. Disclosure is necessary but not sufficient; management plans are the next step. ICMJE disclosure forms [M].
- **Publication ethics.** COPE guidelines; ICMJE authorship criteria; trial registration before enrollment (ICMJE policy since 2005 [M]); FDAAA 801 results reporting [M].

## 5. Informed consent and decision-making capacity

**Elements of informed consent** (classical analysis [M]):
- *Threshold:* competence (capacity) and voluntariness.
- *Information:* disclosure, recommendation, understanding.
- *Consent:* decision and authorization.

**Required disclosures in research** (21 CFR 50.25 / 45 CFR 46.116 [M]) include:
- purpose, procedures, and duration;
- foreseeable risks and discomforts;
- benefits;
- alternatives;
- confidentiality;
- compensation for injury;
- contacts;
- that participation is voluntary and withdrawal is free.

The 2018 Common Rule added a "key information" concise summary at the start of the form.

**Decision-making capacity** (Appelbaum & Grisso [M]). The four abilities, assessed for the specific decision:
1. Communicate a choice.
2. Understand the relevant information.
3. Appreciate the situation and its consequences.
4. Reason about the options.

Capacity is task-specific and can fluctuate. Tools include the MacCAT-T and MacCAT-CR.

**Psychedelic-specific consent issues** (see psychedelic-ethics-issues.md §1):
- consent given *before* an altered state, for events *during* it (touch, session extension, redosing);
- whether consent can be re-affirmed or withdrawn while intoxicated;
- disclosure of personality and belief change (the "ontological" risk);
- uncertainty disclosure that offsets hype;
- the time-limited window between preparation and dosing.

**Advance directives and "Ulysses contracts."** Pre-commitments made while capacitated, for situations in which capacity is reduced. They are discussed in psychiatry ethics and adapted for psychedelic sessions [M].

## 7. Psychiatry ethics

- **American Psychiatric Association**, *The Principles of Medical Ethics with Annotations Especially Applicable to Psychiatry* [M]. It builds on the AMA Principles. Key annotations:
  - **Exploitation.** Sexual activity with a current or former patient is unethical.
  - **The Goldwater Rule** (Section 7.3): no professional opinion about a public figure the psychiatrist has not examined.
  - Confidentiality; dual agency; involuntary treatment.
- **Coercion and involuntary treatment.**
  - Civil commitment standards: dangerousness, and grave disability [M].
  - The least-restrictive alternative.
  - Informal coercion: leverage, threats, inducements.
- **Duty to warn / protect.** *Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California* (Cal. 1976) [M]. Statutes differ by state; some are mandatory, some permissive.
- **Dual agency.** Forensic, employer, or military roles in which the psychiatrist serves someone other than the patient.
- **Epistemic injustice and testimonial credibility** for people with psychiatric diagnoses [M].
- **Vulnerability of psychiatric research participants.**
  - Washout and placebo designs in suicidal populations.
  - The Markingson case (University of Minnesota, 2004) is the paradigm cautionary case: an antipsychotic trial, consent questioned during psychosis, and a participant suicide [M].
- **Psychiatric advance directives** [M].

## 8. Psychotherapy / counseling ethics

- **Core duties:** competence; informed consent to therapy; confidentiality; avoiding exploitation; managing multiple (dual) relationships; record-keeping; termination; supervision.
- **Boundary crossings vs boundary violations** (Gutheil & Gabbard, *Am J Psychiatry* 1993 [M]):
  - A *crossing* is a departure from usual practice that may be benign or helpful (e.g., a supportive hand on a shoulder).
  - A *violation* is a harmful crossing that exploits the client.
  - The **slippery slope**: small crossings precede violations.
- **Transference and countertransference.** They are intensified in altered states and in long sessions.
- **The power differential** and the client's dependency.
- **Touch in therapy.** Contested in mainstream psychotherapy; central in psychedelic therapy (see psychedelic-ethics-issues.md §2).
- **Codes:**
  - APA (psychological) *Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct*: 2002, amended 2010 and 2016 [M]. A new draft code was out for comment through 2025-03-19 [V]. Check whether a new code has been adopted.
  - ACA Code of Ethics: 2014 is in force; a revised code is expected **fall 2026** [V]. Check whether it has been adopted before citing either version.
  - NASW Code of Ethics (2021 amendments [M]).
  - AAMFT Code (2015 [M]).

## 9. Bioethics and justice topics

- **Distributive justice and access.**
  - Cost of drug-plus-session models; insurance coverage; CPT codes.
  - Geographic access: certified sites, rural areas.
  - REMS burdens.
- **Equity in research.**
  - Representation of racial and ethnic minorities, women, and older adults.
  - Helsinki 2024's "responsible inclusion."
- **Indigenous knowledge, reciprocity, and biopiracy.**
  - The **Nagoya Protocol** (adopted 2010, in force 2014 [M]) on access and benefit-sharing for genetic resources and associated traditional knowledge. The United States is not a party [M].
  - **UNDRIP** (2007) [M].
  - Peyote conservation; ayahuasca, iboga, and psilocybin traditions; patents on synthetic formulations vs traditional use.
- **Commercialization and patents.** Polymorph patents (e.g., COMP360) vs public-domain molecules; mission-driven nonprofits (e.g., Usona) vs for-profit models; public benefit corporations.
- **Drug-policy justice.** Racially disparate enforcement histories versus medicalization that benefits a privileged few; the gap between state decriminalization and medical access.
- **Enhancement vs treatment.** Microdosing and wellness use.
- **Neuroethics.**
  - Altering beliefs, values, and personality: authenticity and identity.
  - Cognitive liberty (the right to mental self-determination).
  - Concerns about mind control and suggestibility.
- **Public health ethics.** Harm reduction; diversion; population-level effects. The 2026 FDA guidance allows public-health considerations in benefit-risk [S].
- **Veterans' and military ethics.** Political advocacy by veterans' groups; VA research.
- **Political economy of regulation.** Regulatory capture; executive pressure on an independent scientific agency; speed vs rigor. Treat these as contested political topics: present the competing views fairly.

## 10. Landmark cases (for analogy)

Use these for analogical reasoning. Name them accurately; dates are [M].
- **Tuskegee syphilis study** (1932–1972). Deception, withholding treatment, racial injustice. Led to the National Research Act (1974) and the Belmont Report.
- **Willowbrook hepatitis studies** (1956–1971). Children with intellectual disabilities; coercive admission linkage.
- **Jewish Chronic Disease Hospital** (1963). Live cancer cells injected without consent.
- **Beecher, "Ethics and Clinical Research"** (*NEJM*, 1966). 22 unethical studies in mainstream journals.
- **Jesse Gelsinger** (1999, gene therapy). Conflicts of interest; unreported adverse events.
- **Ellen Roche** (2001, Johns Hopkins hexamethonium study). Inadequate literature review of risks.
- **Dan Markingson** (2004, University of Minnesota). Psychiatric research consent and conflicts of interest.
- **Henrietta Lacks / HeLa.** Consent and commercialization of biospecimens.
- **Harvard Psilocybin Project / Leary–Alpert (1960–63) and the Marsh Chapel "Good Friday" experiment (1962).** Recruitment of students; investigator self-experimentation; publicity. A historical cautionary analogy for psychedelic research [M].
- **CIA MKUltra** (1953–1973). Non-consensual LSD administration. It shapes public and institutional distrust [M].
- **MAPS MP4 Canadian site (2015) / Buisson v. Yensen & Dryer** (see psychedelic-ethics-issues.md §3).

## 11. A structured method for an ethics analysis

1. **Facts.** What is known, uncertain, or disputed? Separate verified facts from source claims.
2. **Stakeholders.** Who is affected and who decides? List participants and patients, clinicians and facilitators, sponsors, regulators, payers, communities, and future patients.
3. **Issues.** State each ethical question precisely (e.g., "Was consent to touch valid if obtained only before dosing?").
4. **Frameworks.** Apply principlism first, then at least one contrasting lens (care ethics for therapy relationships; justice theories for access). Cite the relevant rule or code provision for each issue.
5. **Options.** At least three realistic options, including "do nothing" and the strongest version of each.
6. **Evaluation.** Weigh each option against each framework. Show the trade-offs; don't hide them.
7. **Recommendation.** Give the recommendation, its strongest objection, and the reply. Mark contestable judgments as **ANALYTICAL JUDGMENT**.
8. **Safeguards.** What would make the chosen option more ethical? Monitoring, consent changes, oversight, data transparency.
9. **What would change the conclusion.** The evidence or events that should trigger re-review.
