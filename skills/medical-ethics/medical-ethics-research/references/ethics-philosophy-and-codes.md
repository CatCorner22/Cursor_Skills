## 1. Levels of ethical reasoning

| Level | Question | Example in psychedelic care |
|---|---|---|
| Metaethics | What makes a moral claim true or justified? | Is "a mystical experience is valuable in itself" a claim that can be true? |
| Normative theory | What makes acts right or wrong in general? | Consequentialist vs. deontological views of paternalistic screening |
| Mid-level principles | Which commitments structure practice? | Principlism's four principles; research-ethics principles (Belmont) |
| Professional ethics | What do practitioners owe in their role? | A facilitator's fiduciary duties |
| Codes, regulation, law | What is required, permitted, or forbidden, and enforced? | Colorado Rule 6.6; 21 CFR 50.25 |
| Case judgment | What should be done here? | Whether to continue a session after a participant asks to stop touching |

Moving between levels is the core skill. A code (level 5) is justified by principles (level 3), which are defended by theories (level 2). When a code gives no answer, go up a level.

## 2. Normative theories applied to medicine

**Consequentialism (utilitarianism)** [M]
- *Core:* the right act produces the best overall outcomes. Variants: act and rule; hedonistic, preference, and welfare-list views of the good.
- *Medicine:* cost-effectiveness, QALYs, triage, public health. Peter Singer's work is the best-known modern application.
- *What it sees:* scale, opportunity cost, the people outside the room, including those harmed by delay (the pro-voucher argument).
- *Critiques:* aggregation can sacrifice individuals; QALYs can discount disabled lives; measurement uncertainty; demandingness.
- *Psychedelic use:* expected-value arguments for speed vs. caution; population harms from non-medical use.

**Deontology (Kantian ethics; Ross)** [M]
- *Core:* some acts are required or forbidden regardless of outcomes. Kant's humanity formula: treat persons always as ends, never merely as means. W. D. Ross's *prima facie* duties (fidelity, non-maleficence, justice, beneficence, gratitude, reparation, self-improvement) can be outweighed only by stronger duties.
- *Medicine:* informed consent, truth-telling, the ban on non-consensual experimentation (Nuremberg; MKUltra's wrong).
- *What it sees:* rights, dignity, and the wrong of using a person even for good ends.
- *Critiques:* rigidity; conflicts between duties; little guidance on how to weigh duties.
- *Psychedelic use:* the absolute bar on sexual contact; consent that is not manufactured by an altered state.

**Contractualism** [M]
- *Core:* principles are right if no one could reasonably reject them (T. M. Scanlon). Rawls's justice as fairness: choose principles behind a veil of ignorance.
- *Medicine:* Norman Daniels's *Just Health*; "accountability for reasonableness" for fair limit-setting (publicity, relevance, appeals, enforcement).
- *Psychedelic use:* evaluating the voucher program's process. Were the selection criteria public, relevant, and open to appeal? (psychedelic-ethics-issues.md §7.)

**Virtue ethics** [M]
- *Core:* act as a person of good character would; Aristotle's practical wisdom (*phronesis*).
- *Medicine:* Edmund Pellegrino and David Thomasma ground medical ethics in the healing relationship and name physician virtues: fidelity to trust, compassion, practical wisdom, justice, fortitude, temperance, integrity, self-effacement.
- *What it sees:* rules cannot anticipate every moment in a 6-hour session; character fills the gap.
- *Critiques:* gives weak guidance on hard dilemmas; character is hard to verify.
- *Psychedelic use:* the "self-effacement" virtue directly opposes facilitators who build personal followings.

**Ethics of care** [M]
- *Core:* moral life is grounded in relationships of dependency and responsiveness (Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings, Virginia Held, Eva Feder Kittay).
- *What it sees:* vulnerability, power, the emotional work of care, and the people who do it.
- *Critiques:* risk of parochialism; can underweight justice.
- *Psychedelic use:* integration and aftercare; the ethics of touch understood as care, not technique.

**Narrative ethics** [M]
- Rita Charon's narrative medicine; Arthur Frank's *The Wounded Storyteller*. Meaning is made in stories.
- *Psychedelic use:* participants' accounts of harm (McNamee, Devenot & Buisson 2023) are evidence, not anecdote.

**Feminist and relational bioethics** [M]
- Susan Sherwin; Susan Wolf (ed.), *Feminism and Bioethics*. Catriona Mackenzie and Natalie Stoljar (eds.), *Relational Autonomy* (2000): autonomy is built and sustained in relationships and social conditions.
- *Psychedelic use:* consent under heightened suggestibility and transference is a relational problem, not only a cognitive one.

**Capabilities approach** [M]
- Amartya Sen; Martha Nussbaum; Sridhar Venkatapuram, *Health Justice* (2011). Justice is measured by what people are able to do and be.
- *Psychedelic use:* access is a capability question; a legal right that only the wealthy can use is thin.

**Pragmatism** [M]
- Joseph Fins, Matthew Bacchetta and Franklin Miller, "Clinical pragmatism" (1997): ethics as inquiry, testing proposals against consequences in context.
- *Psychedelic use:* evaluating Oregon's model by its actual results (international-access.md §9).

**Indigenous and relational ontologies** [M]
- Obligations of reciprocity, kinship with land and plants, collective rights over knowledge. The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics). See psychedelic-ethics-issues.md §9.

**Religious traditions** [M]
- Catholic moral theology (the doctrine of double effect; ordinary vs. extraordinary means), Jewish law (*pikuach nefesh*, saving life), and Islamic bioethics (*maslaha*, public interest) shape many patients' and institutions' views. Present them accurately when relevant; do not rank them.

**Theory pluralism.** No theory has won. Good analysis runs the question through several theories and reports where they converge (strong conclusions) and diverge (real dilemmas). This is "reflective equilibrium" (Rawls; Daniels's wide reflective equilibrium) [M].

## 3. Mid-level frameworks and methods

- **Principlism:** Beauchamp & Childress, *Principles of Biomedical Ethics* (8th ed., 2019) [M]: respect for autonomy, non-maleficence, beneficence, justice. Principles are *specified* (made concrete) and *balanced*; grounded in the "common morality."
- **Critiques of principlism:** Clouser & Gert, "A Critique of Principlism" (*J Med Philos* 1990) [M]: the principles are checklists without a theory to rank them. Casuists say principles are too abstract; care ethicists say they miss relationships.
- **Casuistry:** Jonsen & Toulmin, *The Abuse of Casuistry* (1988) [M]: reason from paradigm cases by analogy. The **four topics** (medical indications, patient preferences, quality of life, contextual features: Jonsen, Siegler & Winslade, *Clinical Ethics*) [M].
- **Belmont principles** for research (1979): respect for persons, beneficence, justice [M].
- **Public health ethics:** Childress et al., "Public Health Ethics: Mapping the Terrain" (*J Law Med Ethics* 2002); Kass's framework (*AJPH* 2001) [M]. These cover the least restrictive alternative, proportionality, necessity, and public justification.
- **Global bioethics:** UNESCO Universal Declaration on Bioethics and Human Rights (2005) [M]. Lipworth et al. use it to illustrate the limits of codes (§8).

## 4. Core concepts, with their intellectual history

| Concept | Key idea and sources | Psychedelic relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | Self-governance; Kantian vs. Millian roots; thick (authentic values) vs. thin (non-interference) conceptions; relational autonomy (Mackenzie & Stoljar) [M] | Belief and value change after treatment: whose values count, before or after? |
| **Paternalism** | Overriding a person's choice for their good; soft (when the choice is not fully voluntary or informed) vs. hard; Feinberg; "libertarian paternalism" (Thaler & Sunstein) [M] | Screening exclusions; non-medical models that trust adults to choose |
| **Informed consent** | Disclosure, understanding, voluntariness, capacity, authorization (Faden & Beauchamp, *A History and Theory of Informed Consent*, 1986) [M] | See §9 and consent-review-checklist.md |
| **Transformative experience** | L. A. Paul, *Transformative Experience* (2014): some experiences can be understood only by having them, and they change one's preferences [M] | The consent problem for psychedelics (§9) |
| **Therapeutic misconception** | Research participants mistake research for individualized care (Appelbaum, Roth & Lidz 1982) [M] | High hope plus media hype in psychedelic trials |
| **Clinical equipoise** | Honest professional uncertainty about which arm is better justifies randomization (Freedman, *NEJM* 1987) [M] | Placebo arms when open-label benefits are widely believed |
| **Vulnerability** | Layers of vulnerability rather than fixed groups (Florencia Luna, 2009) [M] | Suggestibility, trauma, dependence on the facilitator |
| **Exploitation and undue inducement** | Exploitation can be mutually advantageous yet unfair (Alan Wertheimer) [M] | Payment, hope, and access through trials only |
| **Conflict of interest** | Circumstances that create a risk that judgment about a primary interest will be unduly influenced by a secondary interest (Dennis Thompson, *NEJM* 1993; IOM 2009) [M] | Advocate-researchers; equity stakes; therapists selling retreats |
| **Fiduciary duty** | The professional's duty of loyalty and care to a dependent party (law-ethics crossover) [M] | A facilitator's duty when the client cannot protect their own interests |
| **Epistemic injustice** | Testimonial and hermeneutical injustice (Miranda Fricker, 2007); prominent in psychiatry ethics [M] | Discounting participants' harm reports; unblinded trials dismissing patients' own accounts |
| **Moral distress and moral injury** | Knowing the right act but being constrained (Jameton 1984); betrayal of deeply held values (Shay; Litz) [M] | Facilitators pressured to cut corners; veterans as a treatment population |
| **Double effect** | A foreseen harm may be permitted if not intended and proportionate [M] | Restraint or safety touch that causes distress |
| **Conscientious objection** | Limits on refusing care for moral reasons [M] | Clinicians who object to psychedelic referral |
| **Medicalization and enhancement** | Treatment vs. enhancement boundaries; medicalization's costs and benefits | Caraccio et al., *Philos Psychol* 2025;38(7):3340–3383 [V] argue for widening the lens beyond the medical model |

## 5. Justice and the health system

- **Theories:** egalitarian (equal shares or equal opportunity: Daniels's fair equality of opportunity), prioritarian (give priority to the worse off), sufficientarian (everyone reaches a threshold), luck egalitarian (compensate for bad luck, not choices), libertarian (rights against coercion) [M].
- **Ecosystem actors and their duties:** regulators (FDA, TGA, Medsafe), payers (CMS, private insurers), professions (licensing boards), sponsors (industry, nonprofits such as Usona), IRBs, journals, patients and advocacy groups, Indigenous source communities, states running non-medical models.
- **Recurring structural questions:**
  1. Who decides what counts as evidence? (regulatory standards; the voucher controversy)
  2. Who pays? (insurance parity: JAMA consensus point 17)
  3. Who profits? (patents; Marks & Cohen on psilocybin patents [M])
  4. Who bears the risk? (trial participants; first adopters in non-medical programs)
  5. Who is excluded? (bipolar disorder, psychosis risk, pregnancy; people who cannot pay)

## 6. Professional ethics and the theory of codes

- **What a profession is** [M]: specialized knowledge, a service ideal, self-regulation, and a social contract in which society grants autonomy in exchange for trust (Michael Davis on codes as conventions among professionals; social-contract accounts).
- **Functions of codes:** aspirational (ideals), educational (teaching norms), regulatory (enforceable standards), protective (of the public and of the profession), and symbolic (signalling trustworthiness).
- **The built-in tension:** codes both hold professionals accountable and protect them. Sadowski, quoting Bob Brecher, calls this the "ambivalence of professionalization" ("Leaning on the Ethical Crutch," *IEEE Technol Soc Mag* 2014;33(4):44–47, 72) [V]. Credit the phrase to Brecher.
- **Structure of modern codes:** aspirational principles (not enforceable) plus enforceable standards. APA's code states this split explicitly, and its draft revision keeps it [V: APA comment portal: principles are "not enforceable"].

## 8. Do codes work? The evidence and the critiques

- **Empirical evidence is weak and mixed.** In business ethics, Schwartz (2001) is an interview study (57 interviews; *J Bus Ethics* 32(3):247–262) of how codes influence behaviour [V: citation]. A tally often attributed to the literature, 19 earlier studies with 8 finding an effect and 9 none, could not be traced to a primary source [M]; do not cite it. In law, Wilkinson, Walker & Mercer (*McGill Law Journal* vol. 45) studied whether Ontario lawyers actually used their code to resolve ethical problems [V: article page].
- **Healthcare critiques:**
  - Lipworth, Kerridge, Montgomery & Komesaroff, "Promoting ethics across the healthcare sector: what can codes achieve?" (*Intern Med J* 2020;50(11):1333–1338; doi 10.1111/imj.15051) [V]: codes face conceptual tensions, power imbalances, organisational barriers, and exploitation (codes used as cover).
  - Komparic, Garon-Sayegh & Bensimon, "The promises and limitations of codes of medical ethics as instruments of policy change" (*Bioethics* 2023;37(4):406–415; doi 10.1111/bioe.13143) [V: citation; the content summary that follows is M until the full text is read], tracing the Canadian Medical Association's code: codes are interpretive and "multivocal," so they often give more than one answer.
  - Essex et al., a 2025 scoping review of comparative healthcare-code studies (*J Adv Nurs* 2025;81(12):8457–8469; doi 10.1111/jan.16857) [V].
- **What the critiques imply for drafting** (patient-protection-code.md §1): make rules specific and enforceable, separate aspiration from obligation, build in independent oversight, give clients the code, and audit compliance.

## 11. Using theory well in a review

1. **Start from the facts and the framing table.** Ethics built on wrong numbers is wrong.
2. **Name the moral question precisely.** "Is it ethical?" is not a question; "May a sponsor present a relative risk without the absolute risk to a lay audience?" is.
3. **Run it through at least three lenses:** principlism plus one consequence-focused and one duty- or relationship-focused theory. Report agreement and disagreement.
4. **Check the paradigm cases** (casuistry): Tuskegee, MKUltra, MP4, the Lykos CRL.
5. **Check what codes and law already require** (ethics-codes-to-borrow.md); a code's silence is a finding.
6. **Give the strongest opposing argument** and attribute it.
7. **Separate the ethical conclusion from the policy recommendation.** Policy needs feasibility and enforcement too (patient-protection-code.md §3).
8. **Label everything.** A theory's verdict is a JUDGMENT; a code's text is a FACT; a commentator's view is a CLAIM attributed to them.
