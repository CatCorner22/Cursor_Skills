# Evidence reshapes the one-way language skill

The research supports most of the skill's rules, but it ranks them differently from the way the skill does: **center-embedded clauses, clinical jargon that looks like everyday English, verbal risk words, and buried warnings cost readers far more than passive verbs do**, and the one experiment that changed passives alone in legal text found no measurable gain in comprehension or recall (Martínez, Mollica & Gibson 2022, secondary). Passives do cost something. Readers misassign who did what in 12% to 26% of passive sentences while active sentences stay near 99% correct, and the cost rises for dyslexic readers, low-working-memory readers, and readers under memory load. So the skill's absolute active-voice rule stands, but on three honest grounds: it discloses the actor, it fixes accountability for each duty, and it protects the higher-risk readers who make up a large share of patients (28% of U.S. adults read at or below PIAAC Level 1). It does not stand on a general claim that every passive harms every reader. U.S. law sets format, translation, and effective-communication duties, but no federal rule requires a reading level or plain language for private or state providers. Two close audits of Ken Adams's *Manual of Style for Contract Drafting* (5th ed.) ("MSCD") find **2 wrong citations, 6 misstatements, 3 overbroad claims, and about a dozen understated rules** in the skill, plus several dozen MSCD tools that the skill omits. Most of these fix easily; the most consequential error turns MSCD's two-part prudence test for vague words ("not too much at stake **and** a commonplace context") into an *or*. The final section turns all of this into graded rule changes.

## Center-embedding and look-alike jargon cost readers more than passives do

**The passive-voice evidence splits by reader and by sentence.** In Ferreira's agent-identification task, readers named the actor correctly in 99% of active sentences but in only 88% of plausible passives and 74% of implausible passives; Christianson and colleagues (2010) replicated the pattern at 90% and 74% (both figures [secondary], from Table 1 of [Riven 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/211519679.pdf)). Riven's own thesis [full text, first ~100k characters] found 19.23% errors on passives, 25.28% on implausible passives, and 22.75% on passives under a six-digit memory load versus 1.89% on actives; first-language and second-language readers did not differ. Riven argues that these errors arise when readers make a judgment *after* comprehension, which challenges the standard "good-enough processing" account ([Riven 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/211519679.pdf)). Stella and Engelhardt tracked 100 adults, half with dyslexia, and found that dyslexic readers made significantly more errors on passive and implausible sentences, and that low working memory predicted errors on passives specifically [abstract only] ([Stella & Engelhardt 2022](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/83249)). People with agrammatic aphasia scored about 66.5% on reversible active and passive sentences together, against more than 99% for unimpaired adults [partial full text]; that study measured test reliability, not a passive-specific penalty ([Mack et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5113948)).

**Legal-text experiments point at structure, not voice.** Martínez, Mollica and Gibson analyzed about 3.5 million words of contracts and then gave nonlawyers original or edited versions: moving center-embedded definitions into separate sentences produced the largest gain, replacing jargon (*lessee* to *tenant*) also helped, and changing passives or capitalization made no measurable difference; recall rose from about 38% for legalese to 45–50% for plain versions [secondary: press reports, no effect sizes] ([ScienceDaily 2022](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/03/220307162035.htm); [MIT News 2023](https://news.mit.edu/2023/new-study-lawyers-legalese-0529)). Their 2024 production study found that laypeople who wrote laws put center-embedding into 48.1% of sentences, against 5.8% when they wrote stories (OR 8.3) [full text] ([Martínez et al. 2024](https://escholarship.org/content/qt3z47k1fp/qt3z47k1fp.pdf)): writers drift toward this structure whenever they feel they write with authority, which describes policy and consent writers exactly. Randall and Graf's plain-English jury instructions raised the share of questions that at least 90% of listeners answered correctly from 30% to 52%, and the largest single gain (61% to 82%) followed a rewrite that cut passive verbs from 5% to 1% of words, but that rewrite also cut presupposed terms, so the design cannot isolate voice [full text] ([Randall & Graf 2014](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.northeastern.edu/dist/f/4582/files/2023/05/2014-Randall-Graf-Ling-meet-Legalese.pdf)). Masson and Waldron redrafted contracts in three stages and found that only simpler words and simpler sentence structure reliably helped; removing archaic words and defining legal terms showed no separate gain, and comprehension stayed low because the concepts themselves stayed hard [abstract only] ([Masson & Waldron 1994](https://scilit.com/publications/56d000a7a93f02741784f9d935268024)). The synthesis: the skill's strongest syntactic lever does not yet appear in the skill as a named rule. The skill mentions "no nested clauses" only under cognitive disabilities.

**Garden paths leave confident false beliefs, and a comma fixes most of them.** In Christianson's experiments, readers who met sentences such as "While the man hunted the deer ran into the woods" kept the wrong reading in roughly a third to a half of trials and held wrong answers with the same confidence as right ones; a disambiguating comma cut errors from 57.3% to 11.5% for one verb class and from 71.9% to 41.7% for another [full text] ([Christianson et al. 2001](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Christianson-et-al.-2001_LingeringInterpretationsGP_Cog-Psych.pdf)). One-way text gives the reader no chance to ask, so this result matters more here than in conversation. It sits beside MSCD's rule that meaning must never depend on a comma (MSCD 12.75–12.78) without conflict: the comma helps the eye, and the structure must carry the meaning anyway.

**Condition order depends on whether the reader knows the goal.** Dixon's drawing and light-panel experiments, which Steehouder summarizes, found that putting the outcome or condition first produced faster reading and fewer errors when the goal varied from trial to trial; with the outcome last, object errors roughly doubled and conceptual errors rose from 5% to 10% [full text, in Dutch, no statistics] ([Steehouder 2000](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/_tij009200001_01/_tij009200001_01_0019.php)). For branching patient instructions ("If your temperature goes above [X], call…"), the reader does not know which branch applies, so condition-first with a comma fits the evidence. That cuts against the skill's R16, which follows MSCD 3.355 and puts a long condition after the main clause. No medical replication of Dixon exists in these notes.

**Negation slows readers, but nobody has tested "Do not X" against a positive rewrite in medical warnings.** Spoken negations lowered visual attentional capacity in three experiments (for example, 36.1 Hz for assertions versus 29.4 Hz for negations), and the cost shrank with repetition but never vanished [full text] ([Banh et al. 2024](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1451309/full)). The same review reports that negation can prime the opposite action [secondary through Banh]. The safety-critical content of most medical prohibitions lives in the prohibition itself, so the defensible inference pairs the two forms ("Swallow the tablet whole. Do not crush it.") rather than replacing one with the other.

**The most dangerous words look ordinary.** Gotlieb and colleagues surveyed 215 fair-goers, 65% of them college graduates: 96.3% understood a negative cancer screening, but only 86.5% understood "Your blood culture was negative," 79.5% understood "unremarkable," 66.5% understood "Your nodes are positive," 20.5% understood that "quite impressive" X-ray findings meant bad news, 11.2% understood "NPO," and 1.9% understood "occult infection" [full text] ([Gotlieb et al. 2022](https://scispace.com/pdf/accuracy-in-patient-understanding-of-common-medical-phrases-2tooi0d8.pdf)). The skill's R5 list already targets these words; the evidence makes R5 one of the best-supported rows in the rubric. Davis and colleagues found that 46.3% of 395 primary-care patients misunderstood at least one of five label instructions (63% of low-literacy patients; adjusted relative risk 2.32), and that correct understanding ranged from 91.1% for "once each day" to 67.1% for "twice daily for seven days" [secondary and abstract] ([MDedge](https://mdedge.com/content/low-literacy-limits-label-comprehension); [AHRQ PSNet](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/literacy-and-misunderstanding-prescription-drug-labels)). A press release adds that 70.7% of low-literacy patients could state "Take two tablets by mouth twice daily" correctly but only 34.7% could show the right number of pills [secondary] ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/494801)). The skill's 46% matches the published paper; this round's notes carried 47% from conference coverage.

**Obligation and risk words need fixed meanings.** Lomotan and colleagues asked 445 guideline developers and clinicians to rate 12 deontic terms: *must* drew a median of 100 with an interquartile range of 5, *may* 37, and *may consider* 33, while *should*, *recommended*, and *required* spread across a wide, overlapping middle band; the authors advise against *consider* because no one can audit it [full text] ([Lomotan et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2982946)). The study never tested *shall*. On risk, only 7 of 180 readers gave an estimate inside the EU's numeric band for verbal side-effect labels such as *common* and *rare*, and verbal labels raised judged severity and lowered intention to comply [abstract only] ([Berry, Knapp & Raynor 2003](https://reading-pure-test.eprints-hosting.org/14144/)); an audit of 50 English leaflets found that only 8% gave numbers [abstract only] ([Carrigan et al. 2008](https://link.springer.com/10.2165/00002018-200831040-00004)). The earlier verified synthesis adds the Cochrane finding that readers understand natural frequencies better than probabilities (Akl 2011, SMD 0.69) and that the *number needed to treat* reduces understanding [earlier synthesis].

**Readability formulas misjudge medical text.** Formula scores correlated with lay difficulty ratings at only 0.10 to 0.41, and formulas scored abbreviation-dense EHR notes as easier than Wikipedia articles that raters found easier to read; counts of medical concepts tracked difficulty better (r ≈ 0.40–0.50) [full text] ([Zheng & Yu 2017](https://jmir.org/2017/3/e59/PDF)). A second study found r = −0.581 between formula scores and Cloze comprehension, a "reasonable first approximation" and no substitute for reader testing [full text] ([Gemoets et al. 2004](https://lhncbc.nlm.nih.gov/LHC-publications/PDF/pub2004025.pdf)). The skill's "readability formula only as a screen" rule rests on solid ground.

## Readers scan first words and skip side panels

**Formatting decides where eyes land.** Nielsen Norman Group's eye-tracking work shows that readers fall into an F-shaped scan only when three conditions hold together: unformatted text, a reader who wants efficiency, and a reader who will not read every word; distinct headings, informative first words, bold key phrases, and lists replace that pattern with heading-to-heading ("layer-cake") or target-hunting ("spotted") scanning [guidance, heatmaps without inferential statistics] ([NN/g 2017](https://nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). Many patient handouts and policies meet all three conditions, so the first two words of each heading, line, and paragraph carry the message. Eye-tracking reviews add that readers skip about 30% of words, mostly short and predictable ones, and fixate longer on long, rare words [full text, review] ([Schotter, Angele & Rayner 2012](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13414-011-0219-2.pdf)). That supports plain-word substitution directly and suggests, without proof, that a critical *not* risks a skip unless it sits where the eye stops.

**Warnings off the main panel go unread.** In a Michigan State eye-tracking study of prescription vials, every participant read the main white pharmacy label, about half looked at the colored auxiliary warning stickers, and 22% looked at no warning at all; older participants noticed and remembered warnings less often [secondary: press release] ([MSU 2012](https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/changes_needed_for_oft_ignored_prescription_labels_says_agbioresearch_scien)). Older adults choosing over-the-counter drugs looked mostly at the package front and rarely read back-panel safety text, and they misjudged drug safety 63% of the time with mock packaging versus 37% with the ingredient name alone [secondary: institutional news] ([UW–Madison Pharmacy](https://pharmacy.wisc.edu/keeping-an-eye-on-medication-safety/)). Shoppers read only about the top five lines of a Nutrition Facts panel, and 33% said they "almost always" checked calories while eye tracking showed 9% doing so [secondary: press release] ([Graham & Jeffery 2011](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/10/111024084634.htm)). That gap means surveys that ask patients whether they read the leaflet overstate reading. For codeine leaflets, a clear headline summary and a highlighted addiction subsection shortened search time (9.1 versus 10.9 minutes, p = 0.029, n = 30 students), and only 21% of 19 collected leaflets displayed the addiction warning well [notes give no access flag; full results reported] ([Zhao et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7432560)).

**Layout moves attention without moving comprehension.** A 2026 eye-tracking study of a 2,000-word privacy policy in three layouts found that layout changed dwell time but not comprehension, which averaged 63% correct in every condition; dwell time predicted accuracy (r = .33), and readers' self-rated carefulness did not match their observed attention [extended abstract] ([Xiao, Wu & Jo 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.13747v1)). Consent forms and notices therefore need comprehension tests, not just better navigation. For graphs, low-graph-literacy readers answered only 32% of questions correctly when a graph's visual shape conflicted with its axis labels, against 50% for high-literacy readers, because they spent less time on axes and titles [full text, author manuscript] ([Okan et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/89159/1/Okanetal2015_JBDM_author.pdf)). The design rule follows directly: the shape of a graph must say what its labels say. The notes found no eye-tracking study of icon arrays.

**Typography effects stay small.** Wider letter spacing shortened fixations but slowed reading from 209.88 to 200.43 words per minute, with fast readers losing the most and comprehension unchanged [full text] ([Korinth et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00444/full)). Among 48 dyslexic readers, italics lengthened fixations, sans-serif and monospaced fonts shortened them without changing reading time significantly, and OpenDyslexic showed no benefit [full text] ([Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013](https://scispace.com/pdf/good-fonts-for-dyslexia-2debffz8dl.pdf)). These results support the skill's ban on italic emphasis and its refusal to prescribe a special font. The notes found no health-context eye-tracking data on line length, leading, justification, capitals, or underlining, and they found none on how clinicians read policies or how long they look at alerts.

## Documents run three to six grades above readers, and rewrites help unevenly

**The readability gap persists across every document type.** Consent templates at 114 U.S. medical schools averaged about a 10th-grade level, and only 8% of schools with a grade-level standard met their own standard [secondary: press release] ([Paasche-Orlow et al. 2003, via EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/745019)). Two decades later, 91% of 5,239 trial consent forms on ClinicalTrials.gov exceeded the 8th-grade level (mean Flesch-Kincaid 10.99; mean length 13.95 pages) [full text] ([Zai et al. 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/DA2A7B2B96FD4F61697BCE319C25ACA4/S2059866124005958a.pdf/unveiling-readability-challenges-an-extensive-analysis-of-consent-document-accessibility-in-clinical-trials.pdf)). Generic surgical consent forms scored at grade 13.9, and **38.4% of their sentences carried a passive verb** [full text] ([Ali et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01039-2.pdf)). State advance-directive forms averaged grade 11.9, and only 5 of 62 reached grade 8 or below [full text] ([Mueller et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/1472-6939-11-6.pdf)). Only 1 of 185 FDA Medication Guides rated "suitable," and adults answered a mean of 52.7% of comprehension questions correctly [abstract and preview] ([Wolf et al. 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11606-012-2068-7)). Hospital financial-assistance summaries that carried a "plain language" label still averaged grade 10.2 [full text page] ([Randall et al. 2026](https://www.ajmc.com/view/readability-of-hospital-patient-financial-policy-documents)). Self-set targets plainly do not enforce themselves, which argues for the skill's mechanical verification step.

**The readers sit far below the documents.** In PIAAC 2023, 28% of U.S. adults scored at or below Level 1 in literacy (19% in 2017) and 34% in numeracy (29% in 2017) [agency press release] ([IES/NCES 2024](https://ies.ed.gov/learn/press-release/u-s-adults-score-par-international-average-literacy-skills-below-international-average-numeracy-and)). About 6.0% of U.S. adults report a current ADHD diagnosis [full text] ([Staley et al., MMWR 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/wr/mm7340a1.htm)), and CDC surveillance puts autism at about 1 in 31 eight-year-olds [secondary] ([Bioethics.com summary](https://bioethics.com/archives/97333)). The earlier verified synthesis adds that patients forget 40–80% of medical information almost immediately (Kessels 2003) [earlier synthesis]; this round's notes could not retrieve Kessels.

**Consent comprehension has not moved in 30 years.** A meta-analysis of 103 studies found that only 52.1% of trial participants understood randomization and 53.3% understood placebo, against about 75% for freedom to withdraw, with no significant change over three decades [full text] ([Tam et al. 2015](https://scielosp.org/pdf/bwho/2015.v93n3/186-198H/en)). Enhanced consent forms produced the largest pooled effect in one review (SMD 1.73) but with extreme heterogeneity (I² = 97%), and the authors note that "no category of intervention managed to have consistent efficacy" [full text] ([Nishimura et al. 2013](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/1472-6939-14-28.pdf?code=633a7fea-27c1-4cc9-a6b2-48daff91b9c2&error=cookies_not_supported)); an earlier review found that long one-on-one discussion worked best and that enhanced forms had "limited success" [abstract only] ([Flory & Emanuel 2004](https://vivo.weill.cornell.edu/display/pubid15467062)). Nishimura's abstract and results text also disagree on the pooled estimates for discussion (0.53 versus 1.03). The defensible reading: a better document helps, a conversation helps more, and the two together beat either alone.

**Plain rewrites raise accuracy, and lawyers prefer them.** Kimble reports that agency staff answered 45% more accurately and 16% faster with a plain contract, and that 1,462 judges and lawyers preferred plain paragraphs by 80% to 86% [full text] ([Kimble 2006](https://www.michbar.org/file/barjournal/article/documents/pdf4article986.pdf)). Lawyers themselves recalled plain contracts better and rated them equally enforceable [secondary] ([MIT News 2023](https://news.mit.edu/2023/new-study-lawyers-legalese-0529)). Pictographs raised immediate recall of mock discharge instructions from 44.28% to 53.51% in a 13-person test, but 7 of the 40 pictographs had recognition rates below 50%, and the same paper cites studies in which pictures did nothing [full text] ([Kim et al. 2009](https://jmir.org/2009/1/e4)). The skill's rule to test every picture with readers fits that spread.

**Large language models simplify well and draft risky replies.** When researchers asked three models to rewrite 60 association handouts at a fifth-grade level, the models cut the mean grade from about 10.1 to between 7.6 and 5.6 and introduced inaccuracies in 0 to 2 of 60 documents, but the authors never measured omissions [full text] ([Will et al. 2025](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e69955)). GPT-4 cut surgical consent forms from grade 13.9 to 8.9 and passive sentences from 38.4% to 20.0%, and a malpractice defense attorney judged all 15 pairs legally equivalent [full text] ([Ali et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01039-2.pdf)). When GPT-4 drafted free-text replies to patient messages, reviewers judged 7.1% of drafts potentially harmful and 0.6% potentially lethal, mostly because the drafts failed to send the patient to urgent care [secondary] ([News-Medical summary of Chen et al. 2024](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20240424/Opportunities-and-limitations-of-using-a-large-language-model-to-respond-to-patient-messages.aspx)). The skill runs inside a language model, so this gap matters to the skill itself: rewriting an approved source carries far less risk than generating new clinical content, which the skill's `[X]` placeholder rule already recognizes.

**Provider-side and ethics-code evidence stays thin.** Abbreviation errors made up about 5% of nearly 30,000 MEDMARX reports in one analysis, and *qd* alone accounted for 43.1% of them [secondary] ([Respiratory Therapy summary](https://respiratory-therapy.com/department-management/clinical/abbreviations-may-pose-threat-to-patient-safety/)). The Joint Commission's 2024 review of 1,411 sentinel events names "inadequate staff-to-staff communication" and "policies not followed" as contributors but gives no percentages, so no one can attribute a share of harm to unclear policy wording [full text] ([Joint Commission 2024](https://www.jointcommission.org/-/media/tjc/documents/resources/patient-safety-topics/sentinel-event/2024/2024_sentinel-event-_annual-review_published-2024.pdf)). Codish and Shiffman open their guideline paper with the claim that "ambiguity and vagueness in clinical practice guidelines reduce the likelihood of clinician adherence" [abstract, first sentence only] ([Codish & Shiffman 2005](https://digital.ahrq.gov/ahrq-funded-projects/improving-guideline-development-and-implementation/citation/model-ambiguity-and)). The notes found no study that ties the wording or structure of a medical ethics code to comprehension or behavior; the skill's ethics-code rules rest on analogy.

## Procedures work when critical steps lead and each term keeps one meaning

**Cognitive load theory supplies the layout rules, with an expert caveat.** Integrating a diagram with the text that explains it removes split attention, duplicating content in two self-contained forms hurts learning, and worked examples help novices but slow experts, an effect the theory's authors call expertise reversal [full text] ([Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5.pdf)). An eye-tracking thesis found that learners with integrated text and graphics switched between the two far more often than learners with separated layouts [repository record] ([Cierniak 2012](https://bibliographie.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/49678?show=full)). The skill's "place each picture next to its text" rule matches. The expertise-reversal effect also predicts that rationale paragraphs and scenarios will slow experienced clinicians, which favors a layered policy: a short rule-and-action layer, with rationale in an appendix. Nobody has tested that layering on policies.

**Controlled language helps complex procedures and non-native readers most.** Simplified English versions of a complex Boeing maintenance procedure raised comprehension and the ability to locate content for 130 engineering students, with the largest gains for non-native readers, but a simpler procedure showed no effect [full text] ([Holmback, Shubert & Spyridakis 1996](https://aclanthology.org/www.mt-archive.info/90/CLAW-1996-Holmback.pdf)). STE builds its dictionary on "one word, one meaning" [standard] ([ASD-STE100](https://www.asd-ste100.org/about.html)), the same principle as the skill's R4 and MSCD 1.57. Minimal manuals helped students learn "faster and better" [abstract only] ([Lazonder & van der Meij 1993](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/f811580e-953a-4312-a1ee-9916b3f98e05)). Information Mapping's own founding trial found that readers preferred the format but learned no more from it (19.56 versus 17.65, not significant) [full text] ([Horn et al. 1969](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/AD0699201.pdf)). When students studied the instructions before they attempted a task, they scored 98% on novel knots, against 82% for students who tried first [full text] ([Eiríksdóttir 2007](https://core.ac.uk/download/4690004.pdf)), which supports putting the full procedure before the first step in time-sensitive patient tasks.

**Checklists work when design and workflow carry them.** In a simulation trial, surgical teams missed 6% of lifesaving steps with crisis checklists versus 23% without (adjusted RR 0.28) [abstract only] ([Arriaga et al. 2013](https://read.qxmd.com/read/23323901/simulation-based-trial-of-surgical-crisis-checklists)). The WHO checklist pilot cut complications from 11% to 7% [secondary] ([MDedge summary of Haynes et al. 2009](https://www.mdedge.com/content/reliance-checklist-cuts-surgical-deaths)), but Ontario's mandated rollout across 101 hospitals changed neither mortality (0.71% versus 0.65%) nor complications [secondary] ([HealthDay summary of Urbach et al. 2014](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/general-health/adoption-of-surgical-safety-checklists-doesn-t-cut-mortality-685665.html)). One U.S. study found 100% documented compliance while observers saw teams complete only 4 of 13 items on average [partial full text] ([Clay-Williams & Colligan 2015](https://qualitysafety.bmj.com/content/24/7/428)). NASA's checklist guidance gives the most specific structural rules: put the most critical items near the start of each task list, because interruptions make later items less likely to happen; make each response state the target value rather than "checked"; split long lists by task; and end with a completion call [full text] ([Degani & Wiener 1990](https://corpora.tika.apache.org/base/docs/govdocs1/252/252553.pdf)). The skill has no checklist rules.

**SOP standards converge on one skeleton and on active voice.** EPA's SOP guidance prescribes purpose, scope, definitions, responsibilities, step-by-step procedure, records, quality control, and references; it calls for "active voice and present tense," detail sufficient for "a person with a basic understanding, but limited experience," and testing by someone other than the writer [full text] ([EPA QA/G-6, 2007](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-06/documents/g6-final.pdf)). ICH E6(R3) requires trial documents that stay "clear, concise and consistent" [full text] ([ICH 2025](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/ICH_E6%28R3%29_Step4_FinalGuideline_2025_0106.pdf)). The skill's provider-facing order (purpose, scope, definitions, duties by role, escalation, documentation) matches. **Findability limits use as much as readability does**: three London NHS trusts each carried 192 to 457 guidelines and policies, too many for staff to comply with [partial full text] ([Carthey et al. 2011](https://www.bmj.com/content/343/bmj.d5283)), and only 37–48% of ICU nurses agreed that guidelines in their EHR proved easy to locate, while 59% used workarounds [full text] ([Sowan et al. 2019](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2019/1/e11846)). The guideline-appraisal tool GLIA names "decidability" (can a reader tell whether the condition applies?) and "executability" (can a reader perform the action without interpretation?) as its critical dimensions, though its validation shows content validity only [abstract only] ([Shiffman et al. 2005](https://www.springermedicine.com/the-guideline-implementability-appraisal-glia-development-of-an-/22434648)). Those two words compress the skill's whole vagueness method.

**Assessment tools agree on the core.** The CDC Clear Communication Index scores "Active voice" in Part A beside one main message, its location, a call to action, common words, lists, and chunks with headings [guidance] ([CDC CCI](https://www.cdc.gov/ccindex/index.html)); the earlier verified synthesis places active voice at item 6 and notes that it applies to the main message and call to action, not to every clause [earlier synthesis]. PEMAT's understandability items include chunking (item 8), informative headers (item 9), and logical sequence (item 10), and actionability item 22 asks for "manageable, explicit steps" [guidance] ([AHRQ PEMAT](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/patient-education/pemat.html)); this round could not see PEMAT's scoring, and the earlier verified synthesis supplies the conventional 70% threshold [earlier synthesis]. ISO 24495-1:2023 applies to technical writing and legislative drafting alike and defers to WCAG for accessibility [abstract only] ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/78907.html)). AHRQ's teach-back tool tells clinicians to ask patients to explain in their own words and to avoid "Do you understand?" [guidance] ([AHRQ Tool 5](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html)); the 19-of-20-studies figure for teach-back comes from the earlier verified synthesis (Talevski 2020), not from this round.

## Federal law sets formats and translation duties, not reading levels

**Effective communication binds every covered provider now.** Title III requires auxiliary aids "where necessary to ensure effective communication" and lists large print, Braille, audio, qualified readers, and accessible electronic formats [law, from knowledge] ([28 CFR 36.303](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-28/chapter-I/part-36/subpart-C/section-36.303)). Title II and Section 1557 go further: the entity must give "primary consideration" to the format the person requests (28 CFR 35.160(b)(2)), and § 92.202 imports that standard for every HHS-funded provider, private or public [law, read this round] ([45 CFR part 92, subpart C](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-A/part-92/subpart-C)). DOJ's guidance addresses vision, hearing, and speech and never mentions cognitive disability or plain language [read this round] ([ADA.gov](https://www.ada.gov/resources/effective-communication/)).

**Web dates moved; nothing else did.** DOJ's April 2026 interim final rule moved the Title II WCAG 2.1 AA dates to 26 April 2027 for public entities with populations of 50,000 or more and 26 April 2028 for smaller entities and special districts, and stated that the amendments "do not alter any other provisions" [law, read this round] ([FR Doc. 2026-07663](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/04/20/2026-07663/extension-of-compliance-dates-for-nondiscrimination-on-the-basis-of-disability-accessibility-of-web)). HHS moved the Section 504 dates to 11 May 2027 for recipients with 15 or more employees and 10 May 2028 for smaller recipients, and wrote that "recipients have an ongoing obligation" regardless [law, read this round] ([FR Doc. 2026-09266](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-05-11/html/2026-09266.htm)). The skill states these dates correctly but omits the population threshold for Title II. WCAG 2.1 AA excludes the AAA criteria on unusual words, abbreviations, and reading level (3.1.3–3.1.5), so the web rules impose no reading level [standard, from knowledge] ([W3C WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)).

**Section 1557's language-access duties survived the 2025 vacatur.** The *Tennessee v. Kennedy* vacatur reached only the gender-identity provisions, and HHS's June 2026 notice states that "the other provisions of the Section 1557 Rule remain in force" [law, read this round] ([FR Doc. 2026-11015](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-06-02/html/2026-11015.htm)). Section 92.201(c)(3) requires a qualified human translator to review machine translation of text "critical to the rights, benefits" of a person or where accuracy matters or the text turns technical; § 92.201(e) bars reliance on a minor child to interpret except briefly in an emergency; § 92.11 requires a notice of availability in English and the state's 15 most common limited-English languages, which the entity must post in physical locations in at least 20-point sans serif type and attach to intake forms, discharge papers, complaint forms, and handbooks; and § 92.8 requires written language-access and effective-communication procedures [law, read this round] ([45 CFR part 92, subpart A](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-A/part-92/subpart-A); [subpart C](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-A/part-92/subpart-C)). Executive Order 14224 and DOJ's 2025 rescission of its LEP guidance changed executive policy, not these regulations [read this round] ([Harvard EELP tracker](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/rollback-doj-rescinded-longstanding-limited-english-proficiency-lep-guidance-following-executive-order-14224)).

**Plain language enters the law only through the individual.** HHS wrote that effective communication for people with cognitive, neurological, and psychiatric disabilities "may require auxiliary aids and services or strategies different from those employed with patients with other disabilities," including extra time and plain-language answers, and it declined to add plain language to its definition only because its list stays "non-exhaustive" [law, read this round] ([89 FR 37522](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-05-06/html/2024-08711.htm)). The Plain Writing Act binds only federal executive agencies and creates no enforceable right [law, from knowledge] ([Pub. L. 111-274](https://www.congress.gov/111/plaws/publ274/PLAW-111publ274.pdf)). The Common Rule requires "language understandable to the subject" and a "concise and focused presentation of the key information" first, but only for research consent [law, from knowledge] ([45 CFR 46.116](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-A/part-46/subpart-A/section-46.116)). For employee-facing policies, Title I lists "appropriate adjustment or modifications of… training materials, or policies" as a reasonable accommodation [law, from knowledge] ([29 CFR 1630.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XIV/part-1630/section-1630.2)), and EEOC names step-by-step instructions, checklists, and pictures [read this round] ([EEOC](https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/persons-intellectual-disabilities-workplace-and-ada)). A plain default therefore cuts the number of individual modifications staff must make; it does not discharge the individual duty.

**Label format rests on advisory federal practice and state law.** The Access Board's best practices for large-print prescription labels call for 18-point bold, sentence case, a non-condensed sans-serif font, 1.5 line spacing, and the highest possible contrast, and state that they carry no force of standards [read this round] ([U.S. Access Board](https://www.access-board.gov/rx.html)). California requires 12-point sans serif for the clustered key information on at least 50% of the label and standardized directions such as "Take 1 [dosage form] at bedtime" [law, read this round] ([16 CCR 1707.5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/california/16-CCR-1707.5)). New York requires chains of eight or more pharmacies to translate labels into up to seven regional languages [law, read this round] ([N.Y. Educ. Law § 6829](https://nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/EDN/6829)). FDA's one-page Patient Medication Information rule remained a proposal on the latest FDA page that the notes could read (April 2024) [agency page] ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/cder-conversations/patient-medication-information-2023-proposed-rule-help-patients-understand-their-prescription)).

## Two Adams audits find two wrong citations and six misstatements

Two auditors reread MSCD chapters 7, 11, and 12 in full for ambiguity and chapters 7, 8 (drafting paragraphs in full), 9 (in part), and 10 for vagueness, plus targeted ranges of chapters 1, 3, 4, 6, 10, 13, 14, 15, 16, and 17. Neither auditor could read the tables and figures, which the PDF extraction dropped (MSCD's wording for *consent not to be unreasonably withheld* at 3.549 sits in one of those tables). All citations in this section come from the user's MSCD PDF through the two audit notes.

**Wrong citations (2).** R10 cites 11.3–11.11 for *each*, *every*, *any*, and *all*, but MSCD covers those words at **11.105–11.118** (ambiguity audit). R13 cites 10.7–10.10 for the ban on numeric dates, but that ban sits at **10.13** (vagueness audit).

**Misstatements (6).** First, R8's fix "repeat, reorder, or enumerate" implies that numbering fixes every modifier problem, but MSCD 12.8 says enumeration alone cannot fix a trailing modifier. Second, R10's fix "*all of*" contradicts MSCD 11.15, which says that adding *all of* to a plural noun changes nothing, and 11.106, which says *all* turns ambiguous with *may*. Third, R16's fix for *notwithstanding* ("*Even if X*") does not appear in MSCD, which instead puts *subject to section X* in the yielding provision and requires a clear statement of how the two interact (13.638–13.643); *even if* works only where *notwithstanding* precedes a fact. Fourth, the grammar note "a condition that uses *must* needs a stated consequence" garbles MSCD 3.386–3.391, which asks for a purpose clause that names what the condition unlocks, not a penalty; and because the skill uses *must* for every duty, *must* can no longer signal a condition at all. Fifth, the vagueness keep test reads "the stakes are low **or** the context is commonplace," but MSCD 7.52 joins the two with **and**, so the skill lets a high-stakes term pass on familiarity alone. Sixth, the skill lists *likely* as a vague word, but MSCD 13.522 says *likely* "isn't vague"; it states an unclear probability, and MSCD's fix (state a percentage, 13.524) belongs in R17.

**Overbroad claims (3).** R16 flags every *subject to*, but MSCD criticizes only *is subject to* plus an abstract noun (3.338) and prefers *subject to section X* to *notwithstanding* (13.638). The imperative note claims MSCD rejects the imperative "only for multi-actor contracts," but 3.138 adds that even one-actor specifications mix categories and that switching moods reads awkwardly. The benchmark technique cites 8.144 for "a named guideline, a written protocol," but 8.144 lists only the benchmarks that courts used to measure efforts (past performance, negotiation promises, industry practice, parallel contracts, the promisor's own conduct); guidelines and protocols extend that list by analogy.

**The skill's own rules, which the skill credits to Adams.** MSCD has no three-part keep test; the skill builds it from 7.48 and 7.52 and adds condition 3 ("no safety-critical action turns on the word"), which the vagueness audit endorses for one-way text because a patient at 2:00 a.m. has no court to settle the meaning. The clinical high-risk terms (*as needed*, *as directed*, *mild/moderate/severe*, *normal*) and the dose-time phrases in R13 have no MSCD entry; they apply 7.30 and 7.46 and USP <17>.

**Understated rules and unlabeled overrides.** The ambiguity audit finds that R9, R11, R12, R13, R15, and two parts of R16 understate MSCD; the vagueness audit adds the efforts row, the *promptly* row, R13's time rules, and the outcome-control rule. The skill omits MSCD's second legitimate ground for vagueness, expedience (7.49), without saying so. It treats *promptly* as high-risk, although MSCD accepts *promptly* as a measure of performance and prefers it to *immediately* (8.140, 10.129). Two drafting defects round out the list: the *promptly* line says "courts read both" after four terms, an antecedent problem under the skill's own R7, and Rewrite step 4 offers *anyone* for an unknown actor, which MSCD 13.30 cautions against in sensitive contexts. ## Conclusion

The evidence moves the skill's center of gravity. The skill treats voice as its first rule and the strongest lever, but the data rank voice behind sentence structure, word choice, number format, warning placement, and testing; the active-voice rule earns its top spot on accountability grounds, not comprehension grounds. That reframing makes the rule stronger, not weaker: a rule that rests on "who must act" survives the Martínez null result, while a rule that rests on "passives confuse readers" does not. The second lesson concerns verification. 92% of medical schools with a readability standard missed it, layout changes attention without changing comprehension, and in one eye-tracking study self-report overstated label reading nearly fourfold, so the skill's mechanical checker and its eye check carry more weight than any single style rule, and the skill should add the same discipline to center-embedding, summary-box drift, and comprehension testing.

The Adams audits show a different pattern: the skill cites MSCD accurately on most points but compresses MSCD's conditional, balanced advice into absolutes, and the compression introduces errors exactly where MSCD hedges (enumeration, *all of*, *notwithstanding*, the prudence test). The fix runs the other way from the evidence fix: on ambiguity and vagueness, the skill needs more of Adams, not less, because MSCD's part-versus-whole and time doctrine targets the very sentences (trigger lists, dose limits, deadlines) where one-way medical readers act alone.

The active-voice conflict deserves one last plain statement. The best controlled evidence says that converting passives alone does not make legal text easier to understand or remember for fluent adults, and the skill should say so in its "Known contradictions" list. The skill keeps the rule anyway for reasons that the evidence does not undercut: a duty without a named actor leaves no one accountable (MSCD 3.14–3.15, 3.336), the CDC index and EPA's SOP guidance both call for active voice, and the readers most likely to misread a passive (dyslexic readers, readers with low working memory, readers under stress, and the 28% of adults at or below PIAAC Level 1) make up a large share of the people who read medical text. A bright-line rule also lets a checker enforce it, which matters when only 8% of medical schools with a readability standard met it.
