# One Way Optimal Language

A style manual for one-way medical text: provider-facing ethics, policy, and clinical documents, and patient-facing medical, pharmacy, and consent materials. It adapts Kenneth A. Adams, *A Manual of Style for Contract Drafting* (5th ed. 2023) ("MSCD"), and it adds the research on how patients read, remember, and act on health information.

Version 1.0 — 9 November 2026. Owner: Blake Reagan.

---

## Part 0. How to use this manual

### 0.1 What this manual covers

This manual governs two document classes:

1. **Provider-facing documents.** Codes and statements of medical ethics, institutional policies and procedures, clinical practice guidelines, order sets, consent protocols, and clinical notes. Clinicians, staff, ethics committees, and compliance reviewers read them.
2. **Patient-facing documents.** Medicine labels and leaflets, discharge instructions, patient education handouts, consent forms, notices of rights and privacy practices, appointment and billing letters, and portal messages. Patients, caregivers, and surrogates read them.

Both classes share one feature: they speak in one direction. The writer cannot watch the reader's face, cannot answer a follow-up question, and cannot correct a misreading. The text must carry all of the meaning by itself. That feature drives every rule below.

### 0.2 How this manual relates to MSCD

MSCD governs business contracts. A contract differs from one-way medical text in three ways that matter here:

- **Negotiation.** Contract parties and their lawyers negotiate the words. A patient negotiates nothing, and a clinician reading a policy seldom shaped it.
- **Audience.** MSCD writes for sophisticated business people and their lawyers (MSCD 1.31, 3.12). One-way medical text reaches every literacy level, every age, and many first languages.
- **Remedy.** A contract dispute ends in a court. A misread medicine label ends in harm.

This manual keeps MSCD's method — choose the category of meaning first, then the verb structure (MSCD 3.3–3.5) — and keeps MSCD's diagnosis of ambiguity and vagueness (MSCD chapters 7, 10, 11, 12). It overrides MSCD wherever the evidence on patients or the one-way setting points elsewhere. Part 10 lists every override and every contradiction between sources.

### 0.3 Three overriding rules

The user who commissioned this manual set three rules. They outrank everything else in it, MSCD included.

1. **Active voice in every clause.** No exceptions for nonparty actors, unknown actors, or focus on the thing that receives the action (contrast MSCD 3.23–3.26). The only text that keeps a passive is text that a law requires word for word (Part 2.1). Part 2 explains the grammar and the tests.
2. **Ambiguity control by formula.** Apply MSCD's principles as a fixed rubric to every sentence. Part 3 sets out the rubric.
3. **Vagueness only where it earns its place.** Replace vague words with numbers, thresholds, events, or named decision makers wherever reasonably possible. Part 4 sets out the method.

### 0.4 Evidence tags

Each rule carries one or more tags so that you can see why it exists and how strongly the evidence supports it:

- **[MSCD x.xx]** — the MSCD paragraph that supports the rule or that the rule adapts.
- **[Research]** — a named study, standard, or regulation (see Sources at the end).
- **[Design choice]** — a judgment this manual makes where no source settles the point. Treat these as defaults that you may revise after testing with real readers.

**Mentions versus uses.** Italic examples, *Before* passages, and lists of banned words show forbidden forms on purpose. The rules govern this manual's own sentences, not the examples it quotes. The checker in Part 2.6 reports these examples as mentions so that a writer can confirm each one.

### 0.5 Terms this manual defines

- **Writer** means the person who drafts or revises a document.
- **Reader** means the person the document addresses.
- **Actor** means the person or thing that performs the action a verb describes.
- **Category** means one of the categories of meaning in Part 5 (duty, prohibition, permission, and so on).
- **Clinician** means any licensed health professional who treats patients.
- **Medicine** means any drug, whether prescription or over the counter. (This manual uses *medicine* everywhere and never switches to *medication* or *drug* for variety — see Rule R4 and MSCD 1.57.)

## Part 1. Characteristics of optimal one-way language

MSCD 1.2 lists twelve characteristics of optimal contract language. Each one carries over to one-way medical text, some with changes.

| # | MSCD principle | One-way adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Not archaic (1.4–1.6) | Drop *aforementioned*, *heretofore*, *said* as a pointing word, *herein*, *pursuant to*. Archaisms tell the reader that they have entered a closed world. |
| 2 | Not legalistic (1.7–1.32) | Drop legal and clinical terms of art that do a plain word's job. In patient-facing text, replace jargon with everyday words; when a technical term must stay, explain it in the same sentence. |
| 3 | Not confusing (1.33–1.34) | Prevent ambiguity (Part 3) and *reader miscues* — moments when the reader guesses the structure of a sentence wrongly and must reread (MSCD 1.34). |
| 4 | No redundancy (1.35–1.55) | No doublets (*any and all*, *null and void*, *each and every*). No *needless elaboration* — listing obvious members of a class (MSCD 1.51). |
| 5 | No repetition (1.56) | Say each thing once. Two statements of one rule invite two versions of it. Patient-facing summaries form the one planned exception: a short key-information box may restate the main instruction (see Part 7). |
| 6 | Consistent (1.57–1.60) | One word for one meaning, one meaning for one word, across the document and, ideally, across the organization. |
| 7 | Concise (1.61) | Use the active voice, strong verbs, and short forms (*if*, not *in the event that*). |
| 8 | Accessible (1.62) | Use headings, numbered steps, short blocks of text, and readable typography (Part 9). |
| 9 | Does not persuade (1.63–1.67) | Inform; do not sell. For consent and decision materials, this principle becomes an ethical duty: present benefits and harms in the same format (Part 7.6). No rhetorical emphasis (*absolutely*, *under no circumstances*, ALL CAPITALS). |
| 10 | Restrained about controlling what the writer cannot control (1.68–1.70) | A policy cannot make a clinician *ensure* that a patient understands. Require the steps that produce understanding instead (Part 6.4). |
| 11 | Serves a useful purpose (1.71) | Cut provisions that restate the law without changing anyone's conduct, unless a regulation requires the statement. |
| 12 | Do not expect simplicity (1.72–1.74) | Clinical content can stay complex; the prose must not add complexity of its own. For patient-facing text, the target reader's tested understanding — not a formula — decides whether the text works. |

## Part 2. The active-voice rule (Override 1)

### 2.1 The rule

Write every clause in the active voice. *Every clause* includes main clauses, subordinate clauses (*if*, *when*, *because* clauses), relative clauses (*that*, *who* clauses), reduced clauses (*the form signed by the patient*), and elliptical clauses (*if swallowed*, *as directed*). The rule also covers headings, table cells, labels, captions, checklists, questions, and fragments.

One limit applies. Text that a law or regulator requires word for word keeps its exact wording. For example, 45 CFR 164.520(b)(1)(i) requires a HIPAA notice of privacy practices to contain a statement that begins "THIS NOTICE DESCRIBES HOW MEDICAL INFORMATION ABOUT YOU MAY BE USED AND DISCLOSED." Keep such text verbatim, mark it, and flag it to the document's owner. The limit gives no permission for any other passive.

### 2.2 The grammar behind the rule

A clause in the **active voice** makes the actor its subject: *The nurse checks your blood pressure.* A clause in the **passive voice** makes the receiver of the action its subject and builds the verb from a form of *be* (or *get*) plus a past participle: *Your blood pressure is checked by the nurse.* A past participle is the verb form that follows *has* (*has taken*, *has checked*, *has given*). MSCD calls the phrase that names the actor (*by the nurse*) the *by-agent* and notes that a writer can drop it — *Your blood pressure is checked* — which hides the actor entirely (MSCD 3.14).

MSCD gives three drawbacks of the passive (MSCD 3.15):

1. With a by-agent, it adds words.
2. It reverses the normal subject–verb–object order.
3. Without a by-agent, it hides who acts.

MSCD's own case example shows the third drawback at work. A noncompetition clause said the restriction ended "if [the employee's] employment ... is terminated"; the passive voice did not say who ended the employment, and a court applied the clause when the employee quit (MSCD 3.16–3.21).

The research adds a fourth drawback for one-way text: readers misread passives more often than actives. In Ferreira's experiments, adults misidentified the actor in passive sentences more often than in active ones, especially when the passive sentence described an unexpected event [Research: Ferreira 2003; Christianson 2010]. A later study of adults with and without dyslexia found that lower-ability readers leaned more on shortcut interpretations of passive sentences [Research: Stella & Engelhardt 2022]. The CDC Clear Communication Index scores a document down unless both its main message and its call to action use the active voice [Research: CDC CCI item 6]. The W3C cognitive-accessibility guidance names active voice as an aid for readers who struggle with implied information [Research: W3C COGA / WCAG 3 draft "clear words"].

Honesty about the evidence: some self-paced reading studies with healthy, fluent adults find that people read passives no more slowly than actives [Research: self-paced reading studies that White Rose eprints 188390 reports]. The case for the absolute rule therefore rests on (a) the hidden-actor problem, which no study disputes, and (b) the greater misreading risk among low-literacy, older, and dyslexic readers — exactly the readers that patient-facing text must reach.

**Step 1 — Flag.** Run the checker (Part 2.6). Then read for the forms below by eye, because no pattern catches every one.

**Step 2 — Decide: action or condition?** For each flag, ask whether the clause reports or requires an action that someone has performed, performs, or will perform.

- **Yes:** the clause is a verbal passive. Rewrite it (Step 3).
- **No:** the participle describes only a condition (*If you feel tired*, *If your ankle looks swollen*, *Are you married?*). Linguists call this an adjectival passive. It behaves like an adjective: it accepts *very* or the prefix *un-*, and it can follow *seem*, *look*, or *remain* [Research: Wasow 1977]. Keep it only if no event is in view and no plain adjective reads better (*You might feel sleepy*).
- **Always an event:** the perfect, progressive, and modal forms (*has been closed*, *is being closed*, *must be closed*). Rewrite them.
- **When in doubt, rewrite.** The rule covers every clause, so doubt resolves toward the active voice.

An earlier version of this manual offered a quick check: add *by [someone]* and see whether the result makes sense. Drop that check. Some adjectival passives accept a *by* phrase (*uninhabited by humans*), and the check invites a writer to keep a passive that merely sounds odd with a *by* phrase.

**Three forms need no rewrite.** A participle directly before its noun works as an adjective inside the noun phrase: *a licensed pharmacist*, *your scheduled visit*, *numbered steps*. A participle inside a hyphenated compound works the same way: *a patient-signed form*. The perfect tense with *have* stays active: *The nurse has given you the dose.*

**Step 3 — Rewrite.** Use the first pattern that fits:

1. Name the actor (*Your doctor will adjust the dose*).
2. Use *be* + a time or place phrase (*Your appointment is on 3 March*; *The clinic is on Main Street*).
3. Use an intransitive verb (*The dose will change*; *The pharmacy closes at 6 p.m.*).
4. For an unknown actor, use *anyone*, *someone*, or *something* (*If anyone loses your sample, we will call you*).
5. Swap subject and object (*Tennessee law governs this policy*).
6. Turn the participle into a noun (*Get a blood test*; *If you have an injury*).
7. Replace the stock phrase (Part 2.4).

### 2.5 Related hidden-actor forms

The passive voice forms one member of a family. Treat the others the same way.

- **Buried verbs (nominalizations).** *Upon completion of the assessment* hides who assesses. Write *After the nurse completes the assessment* (MSCD 17.7–17.10).
- **Buried-actor adjectives.** *payable*, *due*, *eligible*, *subject to*, *responsible for*: *The copay is due at check-in* → *Pay your copay when you check in* (MSCD 3.336–3.338).
- **Fake subjects.** *There is a risk that …*, *It is important that …*. Find the real subject and verb (MSCD 17.24).
- **The verb *receive*.** *You will receive a call* hides who calls. Write *A nurse will call you* (MSCD 3.168).

### 2.6 How to check a document

Use three checks, in this order.

1. **The checker.** The file `active_voice_check.py` reads a document and reports every clause that might break the rule, with a code for the form it found (for example, `PASSIVE`, `REDUCED`, `ELLIPTICAL`, or `STATE?`). It ignores nothing silently: it reports deliberate examples as `MENTION` and required legal text as `MANDATED`. If the computer has the spaCy language library, the checker also runs a sentence-parsing pass.
2. **Decide each hit** with Step 2.
3. **Read by eye** every heading, label, table cell, question, and noun that a participle follows. No automated check catches every reduced clause.

In a Markdown copy of a document, put `<!-- av:mention -->` and `<!-- /av:mention -->` around "before" examples, and `<!-- av:verbatim -->` and `<!-- /av:verbatim -->` around text that a law requires word for word, so that the checker can separate them from your own sentences.

**How well the checker works.** On 9 November 2026, a test set of 496 sentences of medical text measured three tools. The set held 262 sentences with a passive clause, 38 with a condition participle, and 196 fully active sentences.

| Tool | Passives it caught | Active sentences it flagged by mistake |
|---|---|---|
| The single pattern in the first version of this manual | 181 of 262 (69%) | 2 of 196 |
| The checker | 262 of 262 | 0 of 196 |
| The short fallback scan in the skill | 261 of 262 | 18 of 196 |

The checker's rules came from tuning on most of these sentences, so its perfect score overstates its accuracy on new text. On a final set of 55 sentences that the tester wrote only after tuning ended, the checker caught 25 of 25 passives and flagged none of the 26 active sentences. Small samples guarantee nothing; the eye check stays mandatory.

## Part 3. The ambiguity rubric (Override 2)

### 3.1 What ambiguity means

A text is **ambiguous** if it can express two or more inconsistent meanings (MSCD 7.5). Ambiguity differs from vagueness (Part 4) and from four other sources of uncertain meaning — not being specific enough, mistake, conflict between provisions, and failure to address an issue (MSCD 7.3). Clinical guideline research uses the same distinction and finds that both ambiguity and vagueness lower clinician adherence and produce inconsistent practice [Research: Codish & Shiffman 2005].

MSCD's practical lesson: readers fight over unambiguous text too, so aim to prevent misreadings, not merely to achieve technical clarity (MSCD 7.28). In one-way medical text, a "fight" means a wrong dose or a missed warning sign.

### 3.2 How to run the rubric

Run rules R1 through R30 against **every sentence**, in order. For each rule, ask the test question. If the sentence fails, apply the fix. Record each fix in a change log that names the rule and the grammar behind it (see Part 11).

### 3.3 The rules

**R1 — Category first.** *Test:* Which category of meaning does this sentence express — duty, prohibition, permission, absence of duty, recommendation, possibility, statement of fact, rule, condition, or acknowledgment? *Fail signal:* You cannot name exactly one. *Fix:* Choose the category, then use the one verb form Part 5 assigns to it. Never start from the verb. [MSCD 3.3–3.5]

**R2 — Name the actor; use the active voice.** *Test:* Does the sentence name who acts, as the subject of an active verb? *Fix:* Part 2. [MSCD 3.13–3.15, 3.336, 17.7]

**R3 — One action per sentence (patient-facing).** *Test:* Does a patient-facing sentence ask the reader to do more than one thing? *Fix:* Split it, or turn a sequence into numbered steps. Provider-facing text may combine related duties in one sentence when that makes the duties clearer (MSCD 3.43), but never combine two categories in one sentence. [Research: W3C COGA; Design choice for the patient/provider split]

**R4 — One term per meaning; one meaning per term.** *Test:* Does the document use two words for one thing (*medicine/medication/drug*; *doctor/provider/clinician*) or one word for two things? *Fix:* Pick one term, define it if readers need a definition, and use it everywhere. Readers, and judges, assume that a change in wording signals a change in meaning. [MSCD 1.57–1.60, 6.2]

**R5 — Lexical ambiguity.** *Test:* Does any word carry a medical meaning that differs from its everyday meaning? High-risk examples: *negative/positive* (results), *unremarkable*, *impressive*, *progressing*, *stable*, *occult*, *acute*, *chronic*, *discharge*, *culture*, *tolerate*, *compliance*. *Fix:* Replace the word with its plain meaning (*The test found no cancer*), or define it in the same sentence. In a public survey, most adults read *unremarkable* correctly as good news, but only about 1 in 5 knew that an *impressive* X-ray meant bad news [Research: Gotlieb 2022]. [MSCD 7.12–7.15]

**R6 — Abbreviations and symbols.** *Test:* Does the text use an abbreviation, acronym, or symbol? *Fix:* In patient-facing text, spell the term out unless your target readers use the abbreviation themselves; define any you keep at first use. In provider-facing text, define every abbreviation (MSCD 6.9) and never use any item on The Joint Commission's official "Do Not Use" list: *U*, *IU*, *QD*, *QOD*, a trailing zero (*5.0 mg*), a missing leading zero (*.5 mg*), *MS*, *MSO4*, *MgSO4* [Research: The Joint Commission]. Write *unit*, *international unit*, *daily*, *every other day*, *5 mg*, *0.5 mg*, *morphine sulfate*, *magnesium sulfate*.

**R7 — Antecedent ambiguity.** *Test:* Could a pronoun or pointing word (*it*, *this*, *they*, *that*, *such*, *said*, *the same*) point to more than one earlier noun? *Fix:* Repeat the noun. Drop *said* and *such* as pointing words. [MSCD 7.16–7.22]

**R8 — Modifier scope.** *Test:* Does a modifier sit before or after a series of two or more nouns or clauses (*children's apparel, accessories, and footwear*; *debt or capital expenditures over $500,000*)? Does a modifier sit between two phrases so that it could attach to either (a *squinting* modifier)? Could a time phrase attach to the clause before it or after it? *Fix:* If the modifier reaches every item, repeat it with each item or write one sentence per item. If it reaches one item, put that item last when the modifier comes first, and first when the modifier comes last. Numbering fixes a modifier that comes before a series only when the modifier reaches the first item alone; numbering alone does not fix a modifier that follows a series (MSCD 12.8). Never rely on a comma, a semicolon, or the rule of the last antecedent. Recast a long modifier as a separate condition. [MSCD 12.3–12.32, 12.88–12.94]

**R9 — *And*, *or*, *and/or*, and mixed series.** *Test:* Does the sentence use *and/or*, or mix *and* and *or* in one series (*Take A and B or C*)? *Fix:* Replace *X and/or Y* with *X or Y or both*. Resolve mixed series with enumeration or *either … or*. [MSCD 11.90–11.97]

**R10 — Part versus whole.** *Test:* Could a singular noun mean "only one" when you mean "one or more"? Does *any* appear where *a* would also fit? *Fix:* Write *one or more* when the number does not matter and *only one* when you mean exactly one. Replace *any* with *one or more*, *each*, or *every*. Do not add *all of* to a plural noun: MSCD 11.15 says it changes nothing. For *each*, *every*, *all*, and *any* with *may* or with a word of failure, see R24. [MSCD 11.3–11.15]

**R11 — Commas and nonessential clauses.** *Test:* Does the meaning depend on a comma? Does a *which* clause offer an aside? *Fix:* Restructure so the meaning survives without the comma. Use *that* for restrictive clauses. Cut nonessential asides or give them their own sentence. Use the serial comma, but never rely on it. [MSCD 12.44–12.78]

**R12 — Negation.** *Test:* Does the sentence contain two negatives (*not uncommon*, *do not stop unless*)? Does a negative instruction leave the reader without a positive action? *Fix:* State the positive action (*Keep taking this medicine until your doctor tells you to stop*). Remove double negatives. When an ongoing duty ends on a condition, write *except that if X, you no longer have to Y* rather than *unless* (MSCD 3.372–3.385). Readers process negated rules more slowly and less accurately than affirmative rules [Research: negation processing studies; W3C COGA "avoid double negatives"].

**R13 — Time.** *Test:* Does the sentence use *within*, *upon* with an action, *daily*, *twice a day*, *every 8 hours*, *by* a date, *until*, *between*, a numeric date (*3/4*), or *before/after* without saying whether the end day counts? *Fix:*
- Replace *within X days of* with *no later than X days after* (or *before*) (MSCD 10.69–10.71).
- Replace *upon [event], do Y* with a stated period (MSCD 10.57–10.61).
- For medicine timing, separate the dose from the time and name the times: *Take 1 tablet in the morning and 1 tablet in the evening* — not *Take 1 tablet twice daily* [Research: USP <17>; Universal Medication Schedule].
- Spell out the month (*4 March 2027* or *March 4, 2027*; pick one form per organization), and never use an all-number date (MSCD 10.13).
- Say whether the end day counts (*through Friday, 14 May*).

**R14 — Numbers and units.** *Test:* Does the text express a quantity in words, mix words and digits, omit a unit, or use a trailing or missing leading zero? *Fix:* Use digits for every number in patient-facing text, including 1 through 10 (*Take 2 tablets*). Never state a number in both words and digits (MSCD 14.1–14.11). Always give the unit. Use metric units in dosing and match the unit on the label to the unit on the measuring device [Research: USP <17>]. *Note:* this overrides MSCD 14.15, which uses words for one through ten.

**R15 — *Including* lists.** *Test:* Does *including* introduce a list? *Fix:* If the general word already covers the listed items, drop the list. Use *including* only to bring in an item at the edge of the class (*fruit, including tomatoes*). If the list must be complete, write a complete list and drop *including*. Drop *without limitation* and *but not limited to*. [MSCD 13.359–13.384]

**R16 — Exceptions, conditions, and priority words.** *Test:* Does the sentence use *provided that*, *notwithstanding*, *subject to*, *except as provided*, or *may … only*? Does *only* sit before the verb instead of before the word it limits? *Fix:*
- Replace *provided that* with *if*, *except that*, or a new sentence (MSCD 13.698–13.700).
- Put each exception inside the rule it changes and name its source (*Except as section [X] provides, …*), and say how the two rules interact; that is MSCD's alternative to *notwithstanding* (MSCD 13.638–13.643). Write *even if [clause]* only when *notwithstanding* precedes a fact rather than a provision.
- Replace *may … only* with *must not … except* or *The only [thing] that [actor] may [verb] is* (MSCD 3.222–3.226).
- Place *only* directly before the word it limits (MSCD 13.649–13.652).
- Put a short conditional clause first. Put a long conditional clause after the main clause, unless it contains a list; a conditional clause with a list goes first, so that the sentence ends when the list ends (MSCD 3.355, 4.38).

**R17 — Possibility versus permission.** *Test:* Does *may* mean "might happen" anywhere? *Fix:* Use *may* only for permission. Use *might* for possibility, and attach a number wherever one exists (*About 10 in 100 people who take this medicine get a headache*) (MSCD 3.231–3.240). Verbal frequency labels such as *common* and *rare* lead readers to overestimate risk — in one study, people who saw the label *common* estimated a 34% chance for a side effect that numerically occurred in 2.5% of patients [Research: Knapp, Raynor & Berry 2004].

**R18 — Cross-references.** *Test:* Does the text point *above*, *below*, *herein*, *hereunder*, or *the foregoing*? *Fix:* Cite the section by number and heading. [MSCD 7.23–7.27]

**R19 — *Or*: one or both.** *Test:* Could both alternatives that *or* joins happen, and does it matter? *Fix:* Write *X or Y or both*, or *X or Y, but not both*. Do not rely on *either* to rule out both. [MSCD 11.50–11.65, 11.91, 11.93]

**R20 — A negative before a list.** *Test:* Does *not*, *never*, *no*, *cannot*, or *unable* govern a list that *or* or *and* joins? *Fix:* Write one prohibition per item (*Do not drive. Do not drink alcohol.*). If the reader may do one item but not all, say so. [MSCD 11.30, 11.34–11.35, 11.60, 11.66–11.68]

**R21 — Who acts.** *Test:* Does a plural noun or an *and*-pair act, receive an action, or hold a permission? Does *respectively* pair two lists? *Fix:* Write *each* when every member acts alone, *together* when the members act jointly, and *one or more of* when any member may act. Write one sentence per pair instead of *respectively*. [MSCD 11.15–11.35; 13.739–13.748]

**R22 — Trigger lists.** *Test:* Does a list of symptoms, events, or criteria use *and* where the writer means any one item to trigger the rule, or *or* where the rule needs every item? *Fix:* Lead the list with a full sentence that states the quantifier: *Call us if you have one or more of these:* or *You qualify only if all of these apply to you:*. [MSCD 11.41–11.49, 11.96–11.104; 4.39]

**R23 — Shared descriptions.** *Test:* Do two descriptive words share one noun (*pregnant or breastfeeding patients*)? Does *every* or *each* come before two nouns? *Fix:* Repeat the noun with each description, or write *who are both … and …*; add *or both* when a person with both traits counts; write *every X and every Y*. [MSCD 11.36–11.40, 11.83–11.89]

**R24 — *Each*, *every*, *all*, *any*.** *Test:* Does one of them appear with *may*, or with a word of failure (*cannot*, *miss*, *unable*, *not*)? *Fix:* With *may*, write *one or more of them* or *all of them, and not just some*; with a duty, write *every* or *each*, not *any*; with failure, write *one or more* (*If you miss one or more doses*) or *none*. [MSCD 11.105–11.118; 11.9–11.12]

**R25 — Notify: before or after.** *Test:* Does *notice*, *notify*, or *tell us* leave open whether the reader acts before or after the event? *Fix:* *Tell us at least [X] days before you [event]*, or *no later than [X] days after you [event]*. [MSCD 13.615–13.622; 10.65–10.67]

**R26 — The only route; *may require*.** *Test:* Does *may* describe the only way to reach a goal? Does *may require* hide another party's duty or a possibility? *Fix:* If the route is the only one, say so. Replace *[X] may require you to [verb]* with *If [X] asks, you must [verb]*, or with *[X] might ask you to [verb]*. [MSCD 3.227–3.230, 3.241–3.246]

**R27 — Earlier, later, at least one.** *Test:* Does the text pick the earlier or later of two items, or use *at least one of*? *Fix:* *for [X] days or until [event], whichever is later*; *one or more of these*, saying whether you mean groups or single items. [MSCD 13.71–13.86, 13.861–13.869]

**R28 — The slash.** *Test:* Does a slash join words (*nurse/physician*)? *Fix:* Replace it with *or*, *and*, or *or both*; keep a slash only in a fraction or a unit in provider text, and write *per* in patient text. [MSCD 13.927–13.928]

**R29 — Specific enough, and nothing missing.** *Test:* Could a reader ask which kind, how many, how long, or what happens if a common event occurs (a missed dose, a closed office, no surrogate)? *Fix:* Define every category word whose edges change who acts (*with food*, *caregiver*, *adult*); list the common what-if events and answer each one. [MSCD 7.30–7.34, 7.40–7.45, 7.57–7.58]

**R30 — One rule, one place.** *Test:* Does the document state the same rule in two places, or do two sections govern the same event? *Fix:* State each rule once in the body. If a summary box repeats a key instruction, copy it word for word and compare the copies at every revision. When two sections overlap, say which one controls, inside the rule that yields. [MSCD 1.56; 7.37–7.39; 13.638–13.643]

### 3.4 Sentence structure: the strongest comprehension levers

Research ranks sentence structure ahead of voice. Apply these rules with the same force as the active-voice rule.

- **S1 — No center-embedding.** Keep each subject next to its verb. Never put a definition, exception, list, or qualifier between them; give it its own sentence. In legal text, readers recalled more after writers removed center-embedded clauses, while changing passives alone made no measurable difference [Research: Martínez, Mollica & Gibson 2022, secondary source].
- **S2 — A comma after every opener, and meaning that survives without it.** Adding a comma reduced garden-path misreadings from 57% to 12% for one verb class and from 72% to 42% for another, in one experiment with 16 readers [Research: Christianson et al. 2001]; MSCD still warns against letting meaning depend on a comma (MSCD 12.75–12.78).
- **S3 — Condition order.** In patient branching instructions, put the condition first (*If you vomit less than [X] minutes after you take a dose, take another dose.*); goal-first order speeds reading and roughly halves errors unless the reader already knows the goal [Research: Dixon, via Steehouder 2000; weak]. In provider text, follow MSCD 3.355.
- **S4 — One rule per sentence**, and in patient text one action per sentence.
- **S5 — Length.** Aim for 15 words or fewer in patient instructions and never exceed 25; aim for 25 or fewer in provider text. These caps are this manual's standards; no study supports a specific number.
- **S6 — Known words.** A word's frequency and length drive reading time [Research: Schotter, Angele & Rayner 2012]; aim for one new medical concept per paragraph in patient text.

## Part 4. Minimizing vagueness (Override 3)

### 4.1 What vagueness means

A word is **vague** when no sharp line separates cases that meet it from cases that do not, and when meeting it depends on context: *promptly*, *reasonable*, *significant*, *mild*, *regularly* (MSCD 7.46). Vagueness differs from ambiguity. An ambiguous word offers two or more distinct meanings; a vague word offers one meaning with blurry edges.

### 4.2 When vagueness earns its place

MSCD treats vagueness as a legitimate tool when a writer uses it with restraint (MSCD 7.3, 7.48–7.53). Keep a vague term only if **all three** conditions hold:

1. **Lack of control.** The writer cannot set a precise standard because the situation varies too much or depends on someone else's judgment (MSCD 7.48). Clinical judgment often meets this condition.
2. **Low stakes and a commonplace context — both.** Little turns on the term, **and** the setting is common enough that a reader, or a reviewer after the fact, could tell what a reasonable person would do (MSCD 7.52). The third condition below comes from this manual, not from MSCD.
3. **No safety-critical action turns on the word.** Patient-facing instructions about doses, warning signs, and when to seek care never meet this condition. [Design choice]

If any condition fails, replace the vague term.

### 4.3 The replacement method

Work through these techniques in order and use the first one that fits:

1. **A number or threshold.** *Drink plenty of fluids* → *Drink at least [X] cups of water each day.* *A high fever* → *A temperature of [X] °F or higher.*
2. **An observable event.** *If your symptoms get worse* → *If you cannot keep fluids down for [X] hours, or if you have trouble breathing.*
3. **A named decision maker.** When judgment cannot leave the text, say whose judgment governs and how that person records it: *The attending physician decides whether the patient has decision-making capacity and records the reasons in the chart.* [MSCD 7.48; Design choice]
4. **A benchmark the reader can find.** Tie a standard that must stay vague to past practice, or to a named guideline or written protocol with its version and date. MSCD 8.144 lists the benchmarks that courts used to measure efforts; guidelines and protocols extend that list only by analogy.
5. **Freestanding sub-duties beside an efforts duty.** Keep *reasonable efforts* for the goal that the actor does not control, give it a deadline, and state each related task as its own duty, not as the meaning of *reasonable efforts* (MSCD 8.136, 8.140, 8.161). Example: *The social worker must use reasonable efforts to reach the patient's surrogate no later than [X] hours after the attending physician asks. The social worker must also call each telephone number in the chart at least twice, at least [X] hours apart, and must record each attempt.*

Use the placeholders [X] in this manual's examples deliberately: the clinical value belongs to the clinicians who own the document, not to the writer.

### 4.4 Vague-term replacement table

| Vague term | Why it fails | Replace with |
|---|---|---|
| *as needed*, *PRN* (alone) | No maximum, no trigger. | *Take 1 tablet when you have pain. Wait at least [X] hours between doses. Do not take more than [X] tablets in 24 hours.* |
| *as directed*, *use as instructed* | Points to instructions the reader may not have. USP <17> warns against *take as directed* without supplemental directions [Research: USP <17>]. | The directions themselves. |
| *promptly*, *immediately*, *as soon as possible*, *timely* | Courts read both *promptly* and *immediately* as "within a reasonable time" (MSCD 10.115–10.121). | *No later than [X] hours after …*; *Call 911 now.* |
| *regularly*, *periodically*, *frequently*, *from time to time* | No interval (MSCD 10.147–10.151). | *Every [X] months*; *at each visit*. |
| *soon*, *shortly*, *in the near future* | No time. | A date or a period. |
| *reasonable*, *appropriate*, *adequate*, *as clinically indicated* | Leaves the standard to hindsight. | Criteria, a benchmark, or a named decision maker (4.3). |
| *significant*, *substantial*, *major*, *minor* | "Free-floating" vagueness with no anchor (MSCD 9.24–9.27). | A number, or a defined consequence (*enough bleeding to soak a pad in 1 hour*). |
| *mild*, *moderate*, *severe* (pain, symptoms) | Readers rate differently. | A named scale with anchors (*pain of 7 or higher on a 0–10 scale*), or an observable event. |
| *very*, *extremely*, *highly* | No position on any scale (MSCD 13.924). | Delete, or give a number. |
| *make every effort*, *best efforts* | Implies more than "reasonable" without saying what (MSCD 8.133–8.140). | *Use reasonable efforts*, plus a measure of performance and concrete sub-duties. |
| *likely*, *unlikely*, *probable* | Not vague, but an unclear statement of probability (MSCD 13.521–13.526); apply R17. | A natural frequency (*about 3 in 100*). |
| *normal*, *usual* (activities, diet) | Varies by reader. | List the activities or foods. |

## Part 5. The category lexicon: one meaning, one form

### 5.1 Why a fixed lexicon

MSCD's central idea holds that each kind of meaning gets its own verb structure, and that the writer picks the meaning first (MSCD 3.2–3.5). Clinical guideline research confirms the stakes. When health-services professionals rated how much obligation guideline authors intended by twelve deontic terms, *must* scored a median of 100 out of 100 with almost no spread, *may* and *may consider* scored about 33 to 37, and *should*, *is recommended*, *is indicated*, and *should consider* fell in a middle zone with wide, overlapping ranges [Research: Lomotan et al. 2010]. In other words, readers disagree about what *should* demands. The study's authors recommended three distinct levels, recommended that guideline writers tie them to recommendation strength, and advised against *consider* because nobody can audit whether a reader "considered" something [Research: Lomotan et al. 2010].

### 5.2 The lexicon

| Category | Test question | Provider-facing form | Patient-facing form | Never use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Duty** | Must the actor do it, with a consequence if they do not? | *[Role] must [verb]* | Instruction for the patient's own care: imperative (*Take …*). Requirement the organization imposes: *You must [verb] before we can [verb].* | *shall*, *should*, *is required to*, *is responsible for*, *agrees to*, *will be expected to*, *needs to*, *is to* |
| **Prohibition** | Must the actor not do it? | *[Role] must not [verb]* | *Do not [verb].* | *shall not*, *may not*, *cannot*, *shall refrain from*, *should not*, *is prohibited from* |
| **Permission** | May the actor choose to do it? | *[Role] may [verb]* | *You may [verb].* | *can*, *is permitted to*, *is authorized to*, *is free to*, *has the option to*, *may … only* |
| **No duty** | Is the actor free not to do it? | *[Role] does not have to [verb]* | *You do not have to [verb].* | *is not required to*, *need not*, *is under no obligation to*, *may decline to* |
| **Recommendation (strong)** | Do the evidence and the authors' confidence support advising nearly everyone to act? | *We recommend that [role] [verb].* | *We recommend that you [verb], because [reason].* | *should*, *is recommended*, *is indicated*, *consider*, *it is advisable* |
| **Recommendation (conditional)** | Do benefits and harms balance closely enough that choices will differ? | *We suggest that [role] [verb].* | *Some people choose to [verb]. You and your doctor can decide together.* | *may consider*, *could consider*, *might want to* |
| **Possibility** | Might it happen, without anyone choosing it? | *[Event] might [verb]* + frequency | *This might [verb]. It happens to about [X] in 100 people.* | *may* (for possibility), *can* (for possibility), verbal frequency labels alone |
| **Statement of fact** | Is it simply true now? | Present tense, active. | Present tense, active. | *will* for present facts; *shall be* |
| **Rule or automatic consequence** | Does a result follow without anyone choosing it? | Present tense for rules that apply now; *will* for a result that follows a future event. | Same. | *shall*, *is deemed* |
| **Condition** | Does one thing happen only if another happens first? | *If [event], [role] must / may / will …* | *If [event], [instruction].* | *provided that*, *in the event that*, *should [event] occur* |
| **Acknowledgment** (signed forms) | Does the signer accept a fact as true? | *[Party] acknowledges that …* | *I understand that …* | *agrees and acknowledges*, *hereby acknowledges* |
| **Intention** | Can the writer only state what the parties intend, because a third party decides? | *[Party] intends that …* (MSCD 3.491–3.500) | Rarely needed. | *shall be construed* |

### 5.3 Grammar notes on the lexicon

- **Why *must*, not *shall* (Override 4).** MSCD keeps *shall* for business contracts and confines it to "has a duty to" (MSCD 3.89–3.92). MSCD itself concedes that dropping *shall* "might be a good idea" for court rules, statutes, and consumer contracts (MSCD 3.102). One-way medical documents resemble rules and consumer documents, not negotiated contracts. The Federal Plain Language Guidelines and the Office of the Federal Register both direct writers to use *must* for obligations and *must not* for prohibitions [Research: plainlanguage.gov; OFR]. Readers rate *must* as the most consistent signal of full obligation [Research: Lomotan et al. 2010]. Apply MSCD's *has a duty* test to *must*: replace *must* with *has a duty to*; if the sentence stops making sense, you chose the wrong category (MSCD 3.92, 3.120–3.124).
- **Why the imperative for patient instructions (Override 5).** MSCD rejects the imperative for contracts because a contract binds several actors and switching between moods reads oddly (MSCD 3.133–3.140). A patient instruction sheet addresses one actor — the reader — so that objection disappears. USP <17> models its explicit directions in the imperative [Research: USP <17>].
- **Why *may* means only permission.** *May* can mean permission or possibility. Reserve it for permission and use *might* for possibility (MSCD 3.231). *May not* carries three meanings — "might not," "has permission not to," and "lacks permission to" — so never use it (MSCD 3.307).
- **Why *please* never introduces a duty.** *Please* marks a request, and a request implies the reader may decline. MSCD cites a court that read a "please send" clause as precatory, not mandatory (MSCD 13.681–13.682). Use *please* only for true requests.
- **Why *must* for a condition needs a stated consequence.** *Must* alone does not tell a reader whether failure triggers a remedy or simply blocks a result (MSCD 3.386–3.391). State the consequence: *You must sign this form before surgery. If you do not sign it, we cannot do the surgery.*
- **Why not *consider*.** Nobody can tell whether a reader "considered" something, so the word sets no checkable standard [Research: Lomotan et al. 2010]. State the action, or state who decides and on what criteria.

### 5.4 Matching recommendations to evidence strength (provider-facing)

GRADE, a widely used system for grading clinical recommendations, separates *strong* recommendations from *weak* ones (which some panels call *conditional*), and many organizations word them *we recommend* and *we suggest* respectively [Research: Andrews et al. 2013 (GRADE 14)]. Adopt that pair, attach the strength and the certainty of evidence to each recommendation, and keep the pair separate from the duty forms (*must*, *must not*). A recommendation advises; a duty commands. Never let one word do both jobs.

## Part 6. Provider-facing documents

### 6.1 Readers and their task

Provider-facing readers apply the text under time pressure, often at the bedside, and often years after the authors finished. Ambiguity and vagueness in clinical guidelines reduce adherence and produce inappropriate practice variation [Research: Codish & Shiffman 2005].

### 6.2 Structure

Use this order unless a regulation prescribes another:

1. **Purpose** — one or two sentences.
2. **Scope** — which roles, settings, and patients the document covers.
3. **Definitions** — only for terms the document uses in a special sense. Give defined terms initial capitals and use them consistently (MSCD 6.2, 6.7–6.10).
4. **Duties, prohibitions, and permissions by role** — one numbered section per role or per stage of a process.
5. **Conditions and exceptions** — next to the rule they qualify, with *except that* or an *if* clause, never through a remote *notwithstanding* (MSCD 13.631–13.636).
6. **Escalation** — who decides when the rules run out, and how.
7. **Documentation** — what the actor records, where, and when.

Number sections and refer to them by number (MSCD 4.2–4.43, 7.27). Keep blocks of text under about 15 lines (MSCD 4.63). Introduce lists with a full sentence and a colon, and never continue a sentence after a tabulated list (MSCD 4.38–4.39, 4.46).

### 6.3 Name the role, not the building

*Staff*, *the team*, *the hospital*, and *the provider* rarely tell a reader who acts. Name the role (*the attending physician*, *the charge nurse*, *the ethics consultant*). When several roles can act, say so and say which one acts first.

### 6.4 Do not command outcomes nobody controls

A policy that says *Clinicians must ensure that the patient understands the risks* imposes a duty nobody can guarantee. MSCD flags *ensure* as unclear in its implications and warns against duties the actor cannot control (MSCD 3.162, 3.172–3.180). Replace the outcome with the controllable steps:

<!-- av:mention -->
> **Before:** Physicians should ensure the patient understands the risks, benefits, and alternatives.
<!-- /av:mention -->
>
> **After:** Before the procedure, the treating physician must (1) explain the risks, benefits, and alternatives in words the patient uses, (2) ask the patient to explain each one back in the patient's own words, (3) correct and re-explain any misunderstanding, and (4) record in the chart that the patient explained each item back correctly.

Teach-back improved outcomes in 19 of 20 studies in one systematic review [Research: Talevski et al. 2020].

### 6.5 Remove throat-clearing and rhetorical emphasis

Delete openers that add nothing: *It is the policy of [Organization] that*, *It is important to note that*, *Please be advised that* (MSCD 3.27–3.33). Delete emphasis words — *absolutely*, *at all times*, *under no circumstances*, *strictly* — and do not set whole rules in capitals (MSCD 1.64–1.67, 16.29, 16.36). If one rule outranks another, say which one controls.

### 6.6 Clinical notes are now patient-facing

Since the 21st Century Cures Act information-blocking rules took effect in 2021, patients can read their clinical notes through portals [Research: JAMA Netw Open 2026 (Cures Act and judgmental language)]. In a survey of nearly 23,000 note readers, about 1 in 10 reported that something in a note seemed to judge them or offended them [Research: Fernández et al. 2021]. Language in notes also shapes the next clinician: residents who read a stigmatizing version of the same chart note held more negative attitudes toward the patient and treated the patient's pain less aggressively [Research: Goddu et al. 2018]. Apply these rules to notes:

- Record observations, not judgments. *He is narcotic dependent and in our ED frequently* → *He has about 8–10 pain crises a year, and he usually needs opioid pain medicine in the emergency department* [Research: Goddu et al. 2018, as CPSO summarizes it].
- Prefer neutral verbs. *Patient refuses to wear oxygen mask* → *Patient is not tolerating the oxygen mask* [Research: CPSO summary].
- Drop details that carry no clinical value but signal a value judgment.

## Part 7. Patient-facing documents

### 7.1 Who reads them

- In 2023, 28% of U.S. adults ages 16 to 65 scored at or below PIAAC literacy Level 1, up from 19% in 2017; 34% scored at or below Level 1 in numeracy [Research: NCES PIAAC 2023].
- In a study of 395 primary-care patients, 46% misunderstood at least one of five common prescription label instructions. Among patients with low literacy, 71% could repeat *Take two tablets by mouth twice daily* correctly, but only 35% could count out the right number of pills for a day [Research: Davis et al. 2006].
- Patients forget 40% to 80% of what clinicians tell them almost immediately, and they recall about half of what they remember incorrectly [Research: Kessels 2003].
- Within minutes, patients rely on the bottom-line meaning ("gist") of what they heard or read, not on the exact words or numbers [Research: Reyna, fuzzy-trace theory].

Write for that reader.

### 7.2 Voice and person (Override 6)

Address the patient as *you* and the organization as *we*. MSCD prefers the third person for business contracts and finds the first and second person condescending there (MSCD 3.10–3.12). Patient-facing text has one reader, and the Federal Plain Language Guidelines direct writers to use pronouns to speak to readers directly [Research: Federal Plain Language Guidelines]. When a third person acts, name that person (*your pharmacist*, *your surgeon*) instead of using *they*.

### 7.3 Structure

1. **Put the key information first.** Patients recall what they hear first best, and they recall statements they see as important better than others [Research: Ley 1979; Kessels 2003]. For research consent, the revised Common Rule requires a "concise and focused presentation of the key information" at the start [Research: 45 CFR 46.116(a)(5)(i)]. Apply the same principle to every patient document: a short box at the top states what the document is about and the one to three actions that matter most. [Design choice for non-research documents]
2. **Use explicit, labeled categories.** Announcing categories in advance (*I will tell you what is wrong, what tests you need, and what to do at home*) improves recall; merely putting information in a logical order does not [Research: Ley et al. 1973; Kessels 2003]. In print, labeled headings serve this function.
3. **Make headings answer the reader's questions.** *What is this medicine for? How do I take it? When should I call?* [Design choice; consistent with ISO 24495-1, guideline 5.2.4, on headings that let readers predict what comes next]
4. **Number every sequence.** One action per step (Rule R3).
5. **Keep sentences short.** Aim for 15 words or fewer in instructions and never exceed 25 [Research: W3C COGA draft method, "fewer than 25 words"; Design choice for the 15-word target].
6. **Give fewer recommendations.** The amount patients forget rises with the amount they receive [Research: Ley 1979]. The National Institute on Aging advises limiting printed health materials to a few recommendations [Research: NIA].

### 7.4 Instructions

- **Be specific and concrete.** Patients remember specific advice better than general advice [Research: Kessels 2003; Ley 1979]. *Get some rest* → *Stay in bed or on the couch for the next [X] days.*
- **Separate the dose from the time and name the time.** *Take 1 tablet in the morning and 1 tablet at bedtime* [Research: USP <17>; Universal Medication Schedule].
- **State the purpose.** *This medicine lowers your blood pressure* [Research: USP <17> "purpose for use"].
- **Pair each warning with an action.** *If you feel dizzy, sit down right away and call us at [number].*
- **Tell the reader when to seek help, by threshold.** Use Part 4's replacement method.
- **Say what happens next.** Name who will contact the patient, how, and by when.

### 7.5 Words

- Use the words your readers use, and test them with readers (ISO 24495-1; CDC CCI).
- Explain a necessary technical term in the same sentence the first time it appears: *Your doctor wants you to have an echocardiogram — an ultrasound picture of your heart.*
- Watch for words whose medical and everyday meanings conflict (Rule R5).
- Do not use idioms, metaphors, or sarcasm. Readers who interpret language literally — including many autistic readers and many readers working in a second language — can misread them [Research: AASPIRE web guidelines; W3C COGA "use literal language"].

### 7.6 Numbers, risk, and benefit

- **Use natural frequencies** (*about 10 in 100 people*) rather than percentages or probabilities; people understand them better [Research: Akl et al. 2011, Cochrane review].
- **Use absolute risk**, with the baseline. Relative risk reductions look larger and persuade more without improving understanding [Research: Akl et al. 2011; Zipkin et al. 2014].
- **Keep one denominator** throughout a document (all *in 100*, or all *in 1,000*).
- **Do not use the *number needed to treat* (NNT)** with patients; they reduce understanding [Research: Zipkin et al. 2014].
- **Add a visual** — an icon array or a simple bar chart — for any important risk [Research: Zipkin et al. 2014].
- **Never use a verbal frequency label alone.** *Common*, *rare*, and similar labels lead readers to overestimate risk [Research: Berry, Knapp & Raynor 2002–2004].
- **Present benefits and harms the same way.** Using relative numbers for benefits and absolute numbers for harms tilts the decision; that breaches the non-persuasion principle (Part 1, item 9).

### 7.7 Pictures

Pictures that sit next to the text and illustrate it increase attention to and recall of health information, and they help readers with low literacy most [Research: Houts et al. 2006]. Keep pictures simple, put a plain caption next to each one, and test them with the intended readers. Pictures that provoke strong emotion can backfire [Research: Houts et al. 2006].

### 7.8 Consent and acknowledgment forms

- Start with key information (7.3).
- Use *I understand that* for each fact the signer accepts, one fact per line. [Design choice; MSCD 3.485–3.486 prefers *acknowledge* for business contracts]
- Do not treat a signature as proof of understanding. Pair the form with teach-back and record the result (Part 6.4).

### 7.9 Testing

Readability formulas measure word and sentence length, not understanding; ISO 24495-1 defines plain language by whether readers can find, understand, and use the information, "rather than on mechanical measures such as readability formulas" [Research: ISO 24495-1:2023]. Different formulas also score the same text at different grade levels [Research: comparisons of Flesch-Kincaid and SMOG]. Use formulas only as a first screen. Then score the document with the CDC Clear Communication Index and with AHRQ's PEMAT — which treats 70% as the usual threshold for understandability and actionability — and test it with people from the intended audience [Research: CDC CCI; AHRQ PEMAT]. The often-cited target of a sixth- to eighth-grade reading level comes from AMA and NIH recommendations as the literature reports them [Research: multiple readability studies]; treat it as a ceiling, not as proof of clarity.

## Part 8. Accessibility and neurodivergence

### 8.1 The legal floor (status as of November 2026 — verify before relying)

- **Section 504 (HHS-funded providers).** HHS's 2024 rule adopts WCAG 2.1 Level AA for web content and mobile apps. On 11 May 2026, HHS extended the compliance dates to 11 May 2027 for recipients with 15 or more employees and to 10 May 2028 for smaller recipients [Research: 91 FR, FR Doc. 2026-09266].
- **ADA Title II (state and local government, including public hospitals and universities).** DOJ's 2024 rule also adopts WCAG 2.1 AA. A DOJ interim final rule effective 20 April 2026 moved the compliance dates to 26 April 2027 (population of 50,000 or more) and 26 April 2028 (smaller entities) [Research: DOJ interim final rule, 91 FR 20902].
- **Section 1557 (language access).** The 2024 rule requires covered entities to provide a Notice of Availability of language assistance and auxiliary aids in English and at least the 15 languages that people with limited English proficiency most commonly speak in the relevant state, to use qualified translators, and to have a qualified human translator review machine translation of text that is critical to rights or access, where accuracy is essential, or that contains complex, non-literal, or technical language [Research: 45 CFR 92.11, 92.201(c)(3)]. A June 2026 Federal Register notice of a partial court vacatur left these language-access provisions in force [Research: 91 FR 32887, as secondary sources report it].

The underlying duties to communicate effectively did not wait for those compliance dates; the extensions changed only the technical-standard deadlines [Research: DOJ and HHS rule summaries].

### 8.2 Autistic readers

Autistic adults report communication with clinicians as a leading barrier to care [Research: Doherty et al. 2022]. Autistic adults in one study asked for clearer instructions, supplementary written information, a slower pace, and more detail [Research: Swedish study, Autism in Adulthood 2022]. AASPIRE developed its guidelines with autistic users; they direct writers to be specific and precise and to avoid colloquialisms, idioms, and ambiguity [Research: AASPIRE web accessibility guidelines]. Apply these rules:

- Say exactly what will happen, in order, including sensory details (noise, touch, waiting time).
- Give exact instructions: *Sit in the blue chairs by the window and wait for a nurse to call your name* — not *Have a seat*.
- Avoid figures of speech and implied meanings; explain any you cannot avoid.
- Offer the same content in writing whenever you give it out loud.

### 8.3 Readers with dyslexia

Spacing and layout matter more than any special typeface; no single "dyslexia font" helps everyone [Research: Dyslexia Scotland; BDA style guide]. Use left-aligned text, 1.5 line spacing, short lines, and generous letter and word spacing; avoid italics, underlining, and long runs of capitals [Research: BDA style guide 2023; Dyslexia Scotland].

### 8.4 Readers with cognitive or learning disabilities, and people living with dementia

Use common words, simple tense, active voice, literal language, no double negatives, no nested clauses, short text, and chunked content [Research: W3C COGA "Making Content Usable"]. Keep the number of steps small and put each step on its own line.

### 8.5 Readers with intellectual disabilities

Easy Read materials — very short sentences that sit next to pictures — remain the common accommodation, and readers with intellectual disabilities value them. Little evidence, and mostly low-quality evidence, shows that Easy Read improves understanding or outcomes [Research: Chinn & Homeyard 2017; Wilson et al. 2026]. Co-design Easy Read materials with the intended readers, test them, and pair them with conversation and support.

### 8.6 Readers with ADHD

This research did not find studies that test written health materials specifically for adults with ADHD. Apply the general cognitive-accessibility patterns — summaries first, chunking, numbered checklists, and white space — and test with readers. [Design choice; this manual notes an evidence gap]

### 8.7 Older readers

Use 12- to 14-point body text, high contrast, and fewer recommendations per document [Research: NIA/NLM senior-friendly guidance]. USP <17> recommends at least 12-point Times Roman or 11-point Arial for critical label information [Research: USP <17>]. Anxiety and age both reduce recall, so put the key instruction first and repeat it in a summary box [Research: Kessels 2003].

### 8.8 Readers working in another language

Plain source text translates more efficiently and more accurately [Research: ISO 24495-1:2023 introduction]. Avoid idioms and culture-bound examples. Route machine translation of critical content through a qualified human translator (8.1).

## Part 10. Overrides of MSCD and contradictions among sources

### 10.1 Overrides of MSCD

| # | Topic | MSCD position | This manual | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Passive voice | Acceptable when the actor is a nonparty, unknown, or beside the point (3.23–3.26) | Never; every clause active | User rule; hidden-actor risk; higher misreading risk for vulnerable readers |
| 2 | Obligation verb | *shall*, meaning only "has a duty to" (3.89–3.119) | *must* | One-way rules resemble statutes and consumer documents, where MSCD itself accepts dropping *shall* (3.102); federal plain-language guidance; Lomotan 2010 |
| 3 | Absence of obligation | *is not required to* (3.292) | *does not have to* | Active voice |
| 4 | Person | Third person (3.10–3.12) | *you* and *we* in patient-facing text | One reader; Federal Plain Language Guidelines |
| 5 | Mood | Indicative, not imperative (3.133–3.140) | Imperative for patient instructions | One actor; USP <17> practice |
| 6 | Numbers 1–10 | Words (14.15) | Digits in patient-facing text | USP <17> explicit numeric instructions |
| 7 | *Acknowledge* | Use *acknowledges* (3.485–3.486) | *I understand that* in patient-signed forms | Plain wording for lay signers [Design choice] |
| 8 | Contractions | Never in contracts (17.37) | Patient explanatory text may use them; instructions never use negative contractions (write *do not*, *cannot*) | Spelled-out *not* resists skimming [Design choice] |
| 9 | Emphasis | None, absent a legal or bargaining reason (16.23) | Bold for the single most important instruction in patient-facing text | Disparity in sophistication — MSCD 16.23's own exception |

### 10.2 Contradictions among sources (keep these visible)

1. ***Shall* versus *must*.** MSCD defends disciplined *shall* for business contracts; federal plain-language guidance and the Office of the Federal Register direct writers to *must*. This manual follows *must* for one-way text and leaves MSCD's position intact for contracts.
2. **How hard passives are.** Offline comprehension studies show more misreading of passives; some online reading-time studies with fluent adults show no processing cost. The difference appears to depend on the reader and the task. The absolute rule here rests on the hidden-actor risk and on vulnerable readers.
3. **Serif versus sans serif.** MSCD reports that the most readable typeface is the one readers know (16.14). The BDA favors sans serif. The NIA's older print guidance favored serif type for printed body text, while NIA/NLM web guidance favors sans serif. No source here settles the question; test with your readers.
4. **Words versus digits for small numbers.** MSCD (words for one through ten) versus USP <17> (numerals). This manual follows USP for patient-facing text.
5. **Negative framing.** This manual discourages syntactic negation (*do not stop unless*). That rule differs from *framing* (*10% die* versus *90% survive*). A Cochrane review found that negatively framed attribute information improved understanding and that framing had little effect on behavior [Research: Cochrane framing review abstract]. Do not read Rule R12 as a ban on stating harms.
6. **Reading-grade targets.** The literature widely cites AMA and NIH recommendations of a sixth- to eighth-grade level; ISO 24495-1 and the CDC reject formulas as the measure of clarity. Use the target as a ceiling and user testing as the measure.
7. **Easy Read.** Policy and readers favor it; the effectiveness evidence remains thin.
8. **Date format.** MSCD prefers day-month-year; most U.S. readers expect month-day-year. Either works if you spell out the month and use one form consistently.

## Part 11. The one-page checklist

Run this checklist on every document. Record each change in a change log with three columns: **original**, **revision**, and **rule and grammar reason** (for example: *R2 — passive voice; the clause made the dose the subject and dropped the actor; the revision makes the doctor the subject*).

**A. Before you write**
1. Name the document class (provider-facing or patient-facing) and the intended readers.
2. List the one to three actions or facts that matter most.

**B. Every sentence**
3. R1 — Choose the category; take the verb form from Part 5.
4. R2 — Use the active voice; name the actor; remove buried verbs, buried actors, and fake subjects.
5. R3 — Give each patient-facing sentence one action.
6. R4–R6 — Keep one term per meaning; remove lay/medical double meanings; remove unexplained and "Do Not Use" abbreviations.
7. R7–R11 — Fix unclear pronouns; make each modifier's scope clear; remove *and/or*; make part versus whole clear; stop relying on commas.
8. R12 — Remove double negatives; give every warning a positive action.
9. R13–R14 — State times and dates explicitly; use digits and units; remove *within*.
10. R15–R18 — Remove *including* lists of obvious items; remove *provided that* and *notwithstanding*; keep *may* for permission only; cite sections by number.

**C. Vagueness**
11. Test every vague term against the three conditions in Part 4.2; replace any term that fails, using Part 4.3.

**D. Document level**
12. Put key information first; label categories; number steps.
13. State risk in natural frequencies with one denominator; present benefits and harms in the same format.
14. Apply Part 9 (typography) and Part 8 (accessibility).
15. Screen with a readability formula; score with CDC CCI and PEMAT (aim for 70% or higher); test with intended readers.
16. Run a final active-voice sweep: run the checker, decide every hit, and read every heading, label, table cell, and question by eye (Parts 2.3 and 2.6).

## Part 12. Worked examples

### 12.1 Provider-facing ethics provision

<!-- av:mention -->
> **Before:** Physicians should consider involving surrogates in decisions to the extent appropriate and shall ensure that the patient's wishes are respected.
<!-- /av:mention -->

| Problem | Rule |
|---|---|
| *should consider* — unclear obligation; unauditable | R1; Part 5 |
| *to the extent appropriate* — vague, no decision maker | Part 4 |
| *shall* — this manual uses *must* instead | Override 2 |
| *ensure* — outcome the actor cannot control | Part 6.4 |
| *are respected* — passive; hides who respects | R2 |

> **After:** If a patient lacks decision-making capacity, the attending physician must invite the patient's surrogate to each discussion about a major treatment decision. Before each such decision, the attending physician must ask the surrogate what the patient would have wanted and must record the answer in the chart. If the attending physician believes the surrogate's choice conflicts with the patient's known wishes, the attending physician must request an ethics consultation.

### 12.2 Medicine label

<!-- av:mention -->
> **Before:** Take 2 tabs PO BID w/ food prn pain. May cause drowsiness.
<!-- /av:mention -->

> **After:**
> **For pain.**
> Take 2 tablets in the morning and 2 tablets in the evening, with food, only on days when you have pain.
> Do not take more than 4 tablets in 24 hours.
> This medicine might make you sleepy. Do not drive until you know how it affects you.

Rules this revision applies: R6 (abbreviations), R13 (explicit times), R14 (digits), R17 (*might*, not *may*, for possibility), USP <17> (purpose; separate dose and time), Part 4 (a maximum dose limits *prn*).

### 12.3 Discharge instruction

<!-- av:mention -->
> **Before:** It is important that patients be seen promptly if symptoms worsen.
<!-- /av:mention -->

> **After:** Call us at [number] the same day if any of these happens:
> 1. Your temperature reaches [X] °F or higher.
> 2. The redness around your cut spreads past the line we drew.
> 3. You cannot keep fluids down for [X] hours.
> If you have trouble breathing, call 911.

Rules this revision applies: R2 (*be seen* is passive), Part 6.5 (throat-clearing *It is important that*), Part 4 (*promptly*, *worsen*), R3 (one action per line).

## Part 13. Evidence update (November 2026)

A second round of research and two chapter-by-chapter audits of MSCD's ambiguity and vagueness doctrine produced the changes in this Part. The research report "Evidence for one way medical text" gives every source and its access level (full text, abstract only, or secondary).

### 13.1 Why the active-voice rule stands

The rule names who acts, so every duty has an owner (MSCD 3.14–3.15, 3.336). It protects the readers at the highest risk of misreading a passive: readers named the wrong actor in 12% of plausible and 26% of implausible passive sentences against about 1% for actives, and dyslexic and low-working-memory readers erred more [Research: Ferreira 2003 via Riven 2017; Stella & Engelhardt 2022]. It gives a checker a bright line to enforce. The rule does not rest on a claim that every passive hurts every reader: in legal text, changing passives alone did not measurably improve comprehension or recall for nonlawyer adults [Research: Martínez, Mollica & Gibson 2022, secondary source]. Part 3.4 therefore adds sentence-structure rules that carry the same force.

### 13.2 Corrections to earlier parts

- The test for keeping a vague term needs low stakes **and** a commonplace context (MSCD 7.52); the earlier text said "or."
- *Likely* states a probability; MSCD says it is not vague (13.522). Treat it under R17 with a natural frequency.
- Adding *all of* to a plural noun fixes nothing (MSCD 11.15); MSCD covers *each/every/any/all* at 11.105–11.118.
- Numbering alone cannot fix a modifier that follows a series (MSCD 12.8).
- MSCD's alternative to *notwithstanding* is an exception inside the rule it changes, not *Even if* (MSCD 13.638–13.643).
- MSCD uses *must* to hint at a condition and then adds a *to*-clause (3.386–3.391). Because this manual uses *must* for every duty, mark every condition with *if* or *To [get X], [actor] must…*, and state what happens if the reader does not act.
- The ban on all-number dates sits at MSCD 10.13.

### 13.3 Procedures, checklists, and findability

- **Skeleton for a policy or administrative SOP:** control block (title, document ID, owner, approver, effective date, revision date, next review date), purpose, scope, definitions, responsibilities by role, the rule or procedure, criteria or forms, records, references, and revision history [Standard: EPA QA/G-6, 2007]. Cite a checklist at the step where staff use it; the checklist does not replace the procedure.
- **Checklists:** label each list *read-do* (rare, high-stakes, time-pressured work) or *do-confirm* (routine work); keep lists short; put the most critical items first; make each response state the target value (*Nurse confirms heparin at [X] units/kg/h*), not *checked*; end with a completion call [Research: Degani & Wiener, NASA 1990]. Checklist evidence conflicts: crisis checklists cut missed steps from 23% to 6% in a simulation trial [Research: Arriaga 2013, abstract only], while mortality and complications did not change significantly after Ontario required the checklist in every hospital [Research: Urbach 2014, secondary source]; human-factors reviews tie the gap to workflow integration.
- **Findability:** NHS trusts held 192–457 policies each [Research: Carthey 2011], and only 37–48% of ICU nurses found guidelines easy to locate [Research: Sowan 2019]. Title each policy with its subject first, state each rule in one policy only (R30), and name the owner and the next review date.
- **Recommendations** must be decidable and executable, the two critical dimensions of GLIA [Standard: GLIA].

### 13.4 Patient materials: layout, warnings, graphs, and testing

- Put the key message in the first two lines, and start every heading and list item with its two most informative words. Readers scan in an F-pattern only when text has no formatting [Research: NN/g 2017]. Question headings remain an option, not a rule: nobody has tested them against other informative headings.
- Put every critical warning inside the main label or the first panel that readers read. In one eye-tracking study, about half of participants looked at prescription warning stickers and 22% looked at none [Research: Bix 2012, press-release data].
- Make a graph's shape say what its axis labels say. Readers with low graph literacy skip the labels and answered 32% correctly, against 50% for readers with high graph literacy, on graphs where shape and labels conflicted [Research: Okan, Galesic & Garcia-Retamero 2016].
- In consent forms, explain randomization and placebo in plain words and add a teach-back item for each: across 103 studies, only 52.1% of participants understood randomization and 53.3% understood placebo [Research: Tam et al. 2015].
- Test comprehension with a task or teach-back. Layout, dwell time, and self-report do not show understanding: a privacy-policy layout changed dwell time but not comprehension [Research: Xiao, Wu & Jo 2026], and readability formulas correlate with lay difficulty at only r = 0.10–0.41 [Research: Zheng & Yu 2017].

### 13.5 Machine drafting and translation

Rewrite only from an approved source. Check every machine draft for omissions as well as errors: one study found errors in 0–3.3% of rewrites but did not measure omissions [Research: Will 2025]. Route machine translation of critical, accuracy-essential, or complex text to a qualified human translator [Law: 45 CFR 92.201(c)(3)].

### 13.6 Legal floor additions (verify currency before relying on them)

- No federal statute or regulation expressly requires a reading level or plain language for private or state providers' patient materials. The exception is research consent: 45 CFR 46.116(a)(3) requires information in language understandable to the subject, and 46.116(a)(5)(i) requires a key-information summary first.
- Section 1557 applies the Title II effective-communication standard, including primary consideration to the patient's requested aid, to HHS-funded providers (45 CFR 92.202). HHS states that effective communication for patients with cognitive, neurological, and psychiatric disabilities might require different strategies, such as extra time and plain-language answers (89 FR 37587).
- In October 2025, a court vacated only the provisions of the 2024 Section 1557 rule that expand the definition of sex discrimination to gender identity; HHS states that the other provisions remain in force (91 FR 32887). Posted notices of availability need at least 20-point sans-serif type (45 CFR 92.11), and covered entities need written language-access and effective-communication procedures (45 CFR 92.8(d)–(e)).
- WCAG 2.1 AA, the standard in the Title II and Section 504 web rules, excludes the AAA reading-level and unusual-word criteria; adopt them as house standards and label them as best practice, not law.
- The Access Board's 2013 prescription-label best practices (18-point bold for large-print labels) remain advisory. California requires 12-point sans serif for key label items (16 CCR 1707.5).
- ADA Title I might require accommodations for employees who must read and follow policies: step-by-step instructions, checklists, pictures, reading aloud, and extra training time (EEOC; JAN).
