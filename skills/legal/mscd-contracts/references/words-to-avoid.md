## The usages that matter most

- ***should***: never; for *knows or should have known* don't pick a replacement yourself: offer a defined *Knowledge* term with wording for actual knowledge and for knowledge after reasonable inquiry, and ask Blake (MSCD 13.506; house rule 12). In a conditional clause it adds only tentativeness: *If Acme exercises the Option*, not *If Acme should exercise* or *Should Acme exercise* (3.360). In the sense of duty it imposes no obligation; use the verb the category calls for (*shall*, *recommends*).
- **Obligation verbs other than *shall***: *agrees to*, *covenants to*, *undertakes to*, *is obligated to*, *shall be obligated to* (3.125-3.132).
- ***represents and warrants***: *states* on Blake's drafts (3.457-3.458), every instance, a stray one included; on the other side's drafts, suggest the remedies-neutral sentence in a comment (3.459-3.463).
- ***RESOLVED, that*** in a written consent: drop it, and state each action as the board's or the members' act (*The Board approves the Merger Agreement*; house-style.md section 10; MSCD 3.514 differs).
- **Here- and there- words, *said*, *same*, pointing *such***: use *this*, *that*, *it*, or name the provision (by heading, house rule); *hereby* survives only in language of performance.
- **Ambiguity breeders**: *and/or*, *provided that*, *including without limitation*, *notwithstanding*, *for the avoidance of doubt*, *respectively*, *within* a period, *from time to time*.
- **Rhetorical emphasis**: *absolutely*, *strictly*, *whatsoever*, *under no circumstances*, *utmost*, *very* (1.63-1.67).
- **Doublets**: *indemnify and hold harmless*, *right, title, and interest*, *terms and conditions*, *execute and deliver*, *costs and expenses*.
- **Riders**: no ampersands except in business and law-firm names; *if*, not *once* or *when*, to introduce a condition.
- **From the blog**: *also* and *in addition*, *can*, *however*, *because* (recitals excepted).

Judge each hit in context: several words are legitimate in a stated context (*deem* for a legal fiction, *actual* in *actual or alleged*). The table's Rule column says when.

> **Book examples are in MSCD5's style, not house style.** They may use lowercase *schedule*, *1 January 2024*, spelled-out numbers, 1.1 numbering, *the* before party names, or a passive that MSCD accepts. Restate any example in house style (`house-style.md`) before you put it in a contract or a comment. For the other side's drafts, the house rule is to flag everything, whatever a rule's "let-pass" or "style-only" label says.

### Other usage rules and exceptions (242)


### Chapter 1: Contract Language Shouldn't Be Archaic

- **R-1.4-no-archaisms** [sev 3] Don't use archaic usages (for example, whereas in recitals). They add no gravitas and distance the reader from the text. (1.4, 1.5, 1.6; PDF p. 62)

### Chapter 1: Contract Language Shouldn't Be Legalistic

- **R-1.8-manage-terms-of-art** [sev 3] Manage legal terms of art: keep those the transaction needs (for example, security interest, perfection), and avoid the flawed kinds. (1.7, 1.8, 1.9...; PDF p. 62)
  - HOUSE: House addition: A term of art in data/terms-of-art.json keeps its legal meaning; rename any other use (house-style.md section 13). Security: data-security terms (data security, information security, cybersecurity, security incident, security breach, security measures, security controls, security safeguards, security program) and a security deposit stay; flag a bare defined term Security (rename it Security Interest or Collateral) and a physical-safety use (Security Badge, security personnel, security guard).
- **R-1.12-flawed-terms-of-art** [sev 3] Avoid three kinds of flawed terms of art: unnecessary, improvised, and unduly complex. (1.12; PDF p. 62)
- **R-1.13-unnecessary-term-of-art** [sev 3] Don't use a doctrinal term of art where simpler terminology does the job. (1.13, 1.14, 1.15...; PDF p. 63)
  - HOUSE: House addition: A term of art in data/terms-of-art.json keeps its legal meaning; rename any other use (house-style.md section 13). Security: data-security terms (data security, information security, cybersecurity, security incident, security breach, security measures, security controls, security safeguards, security program) and a security deposit stay; flag a bare defined term Security (rename it Security Interest or Collateral) and a physical-safety use (Security Badge, security personnel, security guard).
- **R-1.18-improvised-terms-of-art** [sev 2] Avoid improvised terms of art: ordinary wording onto which lawyers and judges have grafted doctrinal implications, such as hold harmless and best efforts. (1.18, 1.19; PDF p. 64)
- **R-1.20-give-effect-every-word** [sev 2] Expect courts to give effect to every word: that principle, applied zealously, gives significance to stray words and variations and to each element of a redundant string. (1.20, 1.41; PDF p. 65)
- **R-1.21-unduly-complex-terms** [sev 2] Don't use unduly complex terms of art (for example, consequential damages, coupled with an interest, time is of the essence) without fully appreciating their implications; the provision might be unenforceable or have unexpected consequences. (1.21; PDF p. 65)
- **R-1.22-replace-terms-of-art** [sev 3] Use straightforward alternatives to unnecessary, improvised, and unduly complex terms of art. (1.22, 1.27; PDF p. 65)
- **R-1.23-weigh-replacement** [sev 3] Before replacing an entrenched term of art, weigh the risk of confusion against the transaction cost of change; replacement might prompt fruitless debate or seem 'shockingly novel' (for example, states instead of represents and warrants). (1.23; PDF p. 65)
- **R-1.24-explain-term-of-art** [sev 3] To ease a change, use the term of art and also explain what it means. (1.24; PDF p. 65)
- **R-1.25-term-in-heading** [sev 3] Alternatively, use the term of art in the section heading and the clearer alternative in the section text (for example, indemnification, attornment, warranty headings). (1.25, 1.26; PDF p. 65)
- **R-1.28-check-caselaw-first** [sev 2] Before replacing a term of art, check whether courts attribute significance to using that term instead of other wording. U.S. courts tend not to insist on specific wording. (1.28; PDF p. 66)
- **R-1.29-non-english-terms-of-art** [sev 2] If an English-language contract is governed by the law of a jurisdiction whose courts work in another language, include the non-English original of each term of art after the English, in italics and in parentheses. (1.29; PDF p. 66)

### Chapter 1: Contract Language Should Express Ideas Without Redundancy

- **R-1.36-limit-strings** [sev 2] Limit strings of synonyms or near-synonyms (doublets, triplets). If one word expresses the meaning, the rest are extraneous, and a court may give an unexpected meaning to one element. (1.35, 1.36, 1.37...; PDF p. 68)
- **R-1.42-string-variation** [sev 2] If you use a string more than once, use identical wording each time or replace it with a defined term; minor variations invite argument that the meaning differs. (1.42, 1.50; PDF p. 68)
- **R-1.43-prune-synonym-strings** [sev 3] Prune traditional synonym strings, such as books and records and costs and expenses. (1.43; PDF p. 68)
- **R-1.44-subtle-strings** [sev 2] Don't rely on strings with overly subtle distinctions if a different approach would be clearer (for example, alternatives to arising out of or relating to). (1.44; PDF p. 68)
- **R-1.45-improvised-strings** [sev 3] For an improvised string of verbs, pick the word that does the work; deal with any distinct concept (such as delivery) where it belongs. (1.45; PDF p. 68)
- **R-1.46-granting-strings** [sev 3] In granting language, use grants alone rather than a string such as grants, assigns, conveys, mortgages, pledges, hypothecates, and transfers; or, instead of stringing legal functions, refer explicitly to the relevant contexts. (1.46; PDF p. 68)
- **R-1.47-however-referred-to** [sev 3] In an autonomous definition, instead of a long string of synonyms, consider however referred to to show the definition refers to a concept. (1.47, 1.48; PDF p. 68)
- **R-1.49-necessary-strings** [sev 3] Keep a string that is needed to cover the universe of possibilities, even if it includes some redundancy; don't cut it down (for example, to lien) unless you're confident the remaining word covers the rest. (1.49; PDF p. 68)
- **R-1.50-check-each-component** [sev 3] Consider every component of a string and keep it only if its meaning is usefully distinct from each other component. (1.50; PDF p. 68)
- **R-1.51-needless-elaboration** [sev 3] Don't list a general term's constituent components when the general term's scope is clear (needless elaboration). (1.51, 1.52, 1.53; PDF p. 71)
- **R-1.54-obvious-examples** [sev 3] Don't accompany a general term with examples that obviously fall within it, whether introduced by including after the term or placed before it. (1.54; PDF p. 71)
- **R-1.55-redundant-provisions** [sev 3] Omit a provision that another provision makes redundant. (1.55; PDF p. 71)

### Chapter 1: Contract Language Shouldn't Repeat Itself

- **R-1.56-no-repetition** [sev 2] Never say the same thing twice in a contract, including a party's address and a number; repetition adds words and invites dispute if the versions differ. (1.56; PDF p. 72)

### Chapter 1: Contract Language Should Be Consistent

- **R-1.57-one-meaning-one-word** [sev 2] Within a contract, don't use different words or phrases to express the same meaning (for example, the many ways of saying may). (1.57; PDF p. 72)
- **R-1.57-one-word-one-meaning** [sev 2] Within a contract, don't use the same word or phrase to express different meanings (the most abused is shall). (1.57; PDF p. 72)
- **R-1.58-organization-consistency** [sev 3] Across an organization's contracts (and ideally across an industry), draw on the same usages, so no one could tell which individual drafted what. (1.58, 1.59, 1.60; PDF p. 72)

### Chapter 1: Contract Language Should Be Concise

- **R-1.61-concise** [sev 3] Be concise: eliminate archaisms, unnecessary terms of art, redundancy, and repetition, and use other economies of English, such as the active voice and when instead of at the time at which. (1.61; PDF p. 73)

### Chapter 1: Contract Language Shouldn't Seek to Persuade

- **R-1.63-persuasive-words** [sev 3] Be cautious about words associated mainly with expository or persuasive prose, such as therefore, because, and furthermore. (1.63; PDF p. 74)
- **R-1.64-rhetorical-emphasis** [sev 3] Don't add rhetorical emphasis (words that say only 'and we really mean it!') to provisions that already express the meaning; delete it, or for at no time, if and only if, and under no circumstances, replace it. (1.64, 1.65, 1.66; PDF p. 74)

### Chapter 1: Contract Language Should Serve a Useful Purpose

- **R-1.71-useful-purpose** [sev 3] Omit provisions that misunderstand the transaction or the law and so serve no useful purpose (for example, survival provisions and inure provisions). (1.71; PDF p. 77)

### Chapter 2: The Lead-In

- **R-2.160-no-covenant-and-agree** [sev 3] In the lead-in, don't add extraneous verbs such as covenant and agree, don't use hereby, don't say the parties hereby enter into this agreement, and don't name the parties. (2.160, 2.161, 2.162...; PDF p. 112)
- **R-2.164-wrap-into-lead-in** [sev 3] If the body would be a single one-sentence provision (for example, a straightforward amendment), you can wrap it into the lead-in, followed by the concluding clause. (2.164; PDF p. 112)

- **R-2.182-state-actual-consideration** [sev 2] State any actual consideration clearly in the body, not as a recital of consideration; if a transaction (such as a disadvantageous amendment) lacks consideration, arrange consideration and state it. (2.181, 2.182, 2.183; PDF p. 112)

- **R-2.212-no-word-consideration** [sev 3] Dispense with the word consideration and express the exchange accurately, including when payment occurs; for a release, say it is in exchange for a specified action or promise. (2.206, 2.207, 2.208...; PDF p. 112)

### Chapter 3: Language of Agreement

- **R-3.46-no-hereby-agreement** [sev 3] Don't use hereby in language of agreement; hereby belongs to language of performance. (3.46; PDF p. 139)

### Chapter 3: Language of Performance

- **R-3.53-hereby-only-performance** [sev 3] Omit hereby wherever it is used other than in language of performance. (3.53; PDF p. 141)
- **R-3.55-no-now** [sev 3] Don't use now in language of performance: it's primarily oral, a little old-fashioned, and could be read as habitual. (3.54, 3.55; PDF p. 142)
- **R-3.57-no-present-continuous** [sev 3] Don't use the present continuous in language of performance; performance is instantaneous, not an unfolding event. (3.57; PDF p. 143)
- **R-3.58-no-do-auxiliary** [sev 3] Don't use do as an auxiliary in language of performance (does hereby), and don't use doth or hath; they're archaisms. (3.58; PDF p. 143)
- **R-3.59-no-agrees-to-performance** [sev 1] Don't use agrees to, or agrees to and does hereby, as language of performance; it invites a fight over whether the clause is performance or obligation. (3.59; PDF p. 143)
  - Book example: [1-1f] ××: Acme agrees to purchase the Assets from Jackson. → [1-1] ✓✓: Acme hereby purchases the Assets from Jackson.
  - Book example: [1-1g] ××: Acme agrees to and does hereby purchase the Assets from Jackson. → [1-1] ✓✓: Acme hereby purchases the Assets from Jackson.
- **R-3.60-no-by-these-presents** [sev 3] Don't use by these presents, alone or preceded by does, in language of performance. (3.60; PDF p. 143)
- **R-3.61-no-obligation-as-of-signing** [sev 1] Don't try to turn language of obligation into performance by adding (as) of the date of this agreement; it imposes an obligation to act sometime on that date rather than effecting a transfer on signing. (3.61; PDF p. 143)
  - Book example: [1-1i] ××: Acme shall purchase the Assets from Jackson as of the date of this agreement. → [1-1] ✓✓: Acme hereby purchases the Assets from Jackson.
- **R-3.62-no-is-pleased-to** [sev 2] Don't use is pleased to in language of performance; it doesn't make clear that the speech act accomplishes the action. (3.62; PDF p. 143)
- **R-3.63-no-irrevocably-performance** [sev 3] Don't use irrevocably, unconditionally, absolutely, or forever in language of performance; absent contrary language, an action taken can't be undone, so they're redundant. (3.63; PDF p. 143)
- **R-3.64-irrevocably-appoint** [sev 2] Use irrevocably with appoint if you don't want the appointing party to be able to remove the appointee, because the power to appoint might include the power to remove; or say so explicitly. (3.64; PDF p. 143)
- **R-3.65-irrevocable-ongoing-consent** [sev 2] If a party consents to ongoing conduct (not a one-time action), use irrevocably so the consenting party can't later change its mind. (3.65; PDF p. 143)

### Chapter 3: Language of Obligation

- **R-3.125-no-agrees-to-obligation** [sev 2] Don't use agrees to to express obligations; it is clumsy, awkward for future obligations, and litigants have argued it is language of performance. (3.125, 3.126, 3.127; PDF p. 161)
- **R-3.128-no-promises-to** [sev 3] Don't use promises to, even in promissory notes; consistency favors shall pay. (3.128; PDF p. 161)
- **R-3.129-no-undertakes-covenants** [sev 3] Don't use undertakes to, is bound to, hereby binds itself to, covenants to, pledges to, covenants and agrees to, commits to + gerund, or is committed to for obligations; use shall. (3.129; PDF p. 161)
- **R-3.130-no-is-obligated** [sev 2] Don't use is obligated to for obligations; it is long-winded and could suggest the obligation arises elsewhere. (3.130; PDF p. 161)
- **R-3.130-no-shall-be-obligated** [sev 3] Don't use shall be obligated to; it fails the has a duty test ('has a duty to be obligated'). (3.130; PDF p. 161)
- **R-3.131-no-has-a-duty-to** [sev 3] Don't use has a duty to in contract text, despite its role in the has a duty test. (3.131; PDF p. 161)
- **R-3.162-no-procure-ensure-require** [sev 2] Don't use variants of shall cause: shall procure (Commonwealth, legalistic), shall ensure (implications unclear), shall require (could mean a duty to impose a duty), shall see to it that, or shall see to + abstract noun. (3.162; PDF p. 170)
- **R-3.186-obligation-not-covenant** [sev 3] Use obligation, not covenant, including when grouping obligations (signing-to-closing, while debt is outstanding). (3.185, 3.186, 3.187...; PDF p. 175)
- **R-3.190-no-affirmative-negative-covenant** [sev 3] Don't distinguish affirmative and negative covenants, and don't refer to obligations and prohibitions; obligation covers both doing and not doing. (3.190, 3.191; PDF p. 175)
- **R-3.194-no-adjectives-obligation** [sev 3] Don't modify obligations with irrevocable, absolute, or unconditional. (3.194; PDF p. 175)
- **R-3.195-comply-with-not-perform** [sev 3] Use comply with and compliance with for obligations, not perform, performance, discharge, or fulfill; an obligation can be a duty not to act. (3.195, 3.196; PDF p. 175)
- **R-3.199-breach-mass-noun** [sev 3] Prefer breach to violation, nonperformance, or noncompliance; use breach as a mass noun (no article) unless referring to multiple breaches. (3.197, 3.198, 3.199; PDF p. 175)
- **R-3.200-breach-verb** [sev 3] Where you can, use the verb breach instead of the noun; it's more concise and keeps the actor (the mass noun can drop it). (3.200, 3.201, 3.202...; PDF p. 175)

### Chapter 3: Language of Discretion

- **R-3.249-no-sole-discretion** [sev 2] Don't use at its sole discretion with may. If it's rhetorical emphasis, delete it; if it tries to address a real issue (such as the implied duty of good faith), address that issue directly. (3.249, 3.250, 3.251...; PDF p. 190)
- **R-3.253-no-sole-and-absolute** [sev 2] Don't use absolute instead of or with sole (sole and absolute), or uncontrolled; it's legalistic redundancy. (3.253; PDF p. 190)

### Chapter 3: Language of Prohibition

- **R-3.317-jury-waiver** [sev 3] For jury-trial waivers, you could use prohibition (Each party shall not demand a trial by jury), but because waiver language is universal, you might find it expedient to keep it. (3.317; PDF p. 206)
- **R-3.319-no-shall-never** [sev 2] Don't use shall never; if an obligation should continue perpetually, even after termination, say so explicitly. (3.319; PDF p. 206)
- **R-3.320-no-shall-in-no-way** [sev 3] Don't use shall in no way; it's rhetorical emphasis. (3.320; PDF p. 206)
- **R-3.321-no-cannot-agrees-not** [sev 3] Don't use cannot or agrees not to in language of prohibition. (3.321; PDF p. 206)

### Chapter 7: Sources of Uncertain Meaning

- **R-7.16-antecedent-ambiguity** [sev 1] Antecedent ambiguity: a pronoun or other referring word or phrase (said, such, thereof, it, those, the fee) for which two or more antecedents present themselves. (7.16, 7.17, 7.18...; PDF p. 364)
  - Look for: a referring word whose antecedent could be either of two or more earlier nouns or noun phrases
  - Test: For each referring word, list every earlier noun phrase it could point to; if more than one fits grammatically and sensibly, it is ambiguous.
  - Fix: Make clear which antecedent you mean: repeat the noun, use a defined term, or restructure. Replace archaic referring words (said, thereof, such meaning those).
  - Book example: The defendant agrees to pay for one-half the cost of Sarah's college educational expenses ... subject to the limitation that said cost shall not exceed the tuition for a full-time residential student at UCONN-Storrs. → The defendant shall pay half the cost of Sarah's college educational expenses for a four-year degree net of any scholarships and grants, up to an amount equal to half the tuition for a full-time residential student at UCONN-Storrs.
- **R-7.23-contract-reference-ambiguity** [sev 1] Contract-reference ambiguity: here-words (herein, hereunder, hereof, hereto, hereinbelow) and pointing words (above, below, the foregoing, as aforesaid) leave unclear whether they refer to part of the contract or the whole, or which part; hereunder can also be read as "below" rather than "by operation of." (7.23, 7.24, 7.25...; PDF p. 366)
  - HOUSE: partly superseded: House rule 4: where a reference is needed, name the provision by its heading, not its number (the section titled "Payment"); better, restructure so no reference is needed.
  - Look for: a here-word or pointing word instead of a reference to a specific section
  - Test: Ask whether the word could mean the whole contract, the current section, or the text immediately before or after; if more than one reading is possible, it is ambiguous.
  - Fix: Refer to the whole contract as this agreement or to a specific section by number (section 4.2); use hereby only in language of performance.
- **R-7.26-specific-section-refs** [sev 1] When pointing to other parts of a contract, refer to specific sections, not above, below, the foregoing, or as aforesaid. (7.26, 7.27; PDF p. 367)
- **R-7.27-no-here-words** [sev 1] Don't use here-words, except hereby in language of performance. (7.27; PDF p. 367)
  - Fix: Use this agreement, or a specific section reference.

### Chapter 13: Selected Usages

- **R-13.5-actively-levels** [sev 2] If you use actively to distinguish levels of activity (as in while actively employed, to exclude an employee on leave), instead state the distinction explicitly. (13.5; PDF p. 577)
- **R-13.7-actually** [sev 3] Delete actually; it is rhetorical emphasis and can be deleted without affecting meaning. (13.7; PDF p. 577)
- **R-13.9-affirmative-vote** [sev 2] Instead of the affirmative vote of a majority of the outstanding shares, say what is meant: by vote of the shareholders in which a majority of the outstanding shares vote in favor of the action. (13.9; PDF p. 578)
- **R-13.10-affirmative-action** [sev 3] In affirmative action, affirmative is redundant unless you are referring to employment or higher-education admissions policies; delete it otherwise. (13.10; PDF p. 578)
- **R-13.11-affirmative-covenants** [sev 3] Don't use Affirmative Covenants as a heading; Certain Obligations would be an improvement. (13.11; PDF p. 579)
- **R-13.18-inter-alia** [sev 3] Don't use the Latin inter alia. (13.18; PDF p. 580)
- **R-13.30-anyone-sensitive** [sev 3] Don't use anyone in a sensitive context, because there is little relevant caselaw; use a defined term such as Person instead. (13.30; PDF p. 583)
- **R-13.34-applicable-precision** [sev 2] Don't use applicable if greater precision is required; restate the provision so it is clear which item is meant. (13.34; PDF p. 584)
- **R-13.35-be-applicable** [sev 3] Generally, instead of be applicable, use the verb apply. (13.35; PDF p. 584)
- **R-13.36-as-applicable** [sev 3] You may use as applicable; it is unobjectionable and more concise than as the case may be. (13.36; PDF p. 585)
- **R-13.48-subject-matter-of-this-agreement** [sev 2] Say what the subject matter is rather than arising out of the subject matter of this agreement; that phrase trades one nonspecificity for another and has generated litigation. (13.48, 13.49; PDF p. 588)
- **R-13.51-exclude-extracontractual** [sev 1] If you want to limit a dispute-resolution provision to claims under the contract, include a provision excluding extracontractual liability; don't assume arising out of alone does it. (13.51, 13.52; PDF p. 589)
  - Look for: dispute-resolution provision meant to cover only contract claims
  - Fix: Add an express exclusion of extracontractual (tort, statutory) claims.
- **R-13.58-as-in-effect** [sev 1] When referring to an element of a statute or another contract (for example, a definition), make clear whether you mean it as in effect on the date of this agreement (to freeze it) or as then in effect (to pick up later amendments); if that leaves room for confusion, be more specific (as in effect on exercise of the Option). (13.58, 13.59; PDF p. 590)
  - Look for: cross-reference to a definition or provision of a statute or other contract
  - Test: Can a reader tell which version of the referenced text applies at the relevant time?
  - Fix: as in effect on the date of this agreement / as then in effect / as in effect on [event]
- **R-13.61-as-between-nonparty** [sev 2] If you are tempted to use as between the parties because a nonparty might somehow be implicated, address the nonparty issue explicitly instead. (13.61; PDF p. 591)
- **R-13.70-as-the-case-may-be-misuse** [sev 3] Don't use as the case may be after simple alternatives; that is a misuse. (13.70; PDF p. 594)
- **R-13.73-at-least-one-of-categories** [sev 1] If at least one of refers to categories, or to units within categories, say so explicitly, and say whether you mean units in all categories or in fewer than all; if it refers to units, nothing more is needed. (13.73, 13.77, 13.85; PDF p. 594)
  - Look for: at least one of before a list of categories that each contain units
  - Test: Could one of mean one item within a category or one category? If so, the provision is ambiguous.
  - Fix: at least 1 of the following categories: ...; at least 1 unit in each of the following categories: ...; at least 1 unit in [at least] 1 of the following categories: ...
- **R-13.89-attornment-heading** [sev 3] If you feel you must refer to attornment, use Attornment only as a section heading, with consent in the provision itself. (13.89; PDF p. 599)
- **R-13.96-automatically-legal-fiction** [sev 3] Don't use automatically where a legal fiction is being established; use deem. (13.96; PDF p. 600)
- **R-13.112-accounting-records** [sev 2] To capture the narrower accounting meaning, use accounting records (the UK Companies Act 2006 term) or financial records; a narrower term may spare a party a surprise when the other side asks to audit many kinds of records. (13.112, 13.113; PDF p. 604)
- **R-13.116-both-lengthy** [sev 3] When the two linked items are lengthy, both (or either) can signal in advance that the link is and (or exclusive or); but restructuring the provision might be a better fix. (13.116, 13.117; PDF p. 604)
- **R-13.141-cognizant-agency** [sev 3] Use cognizant agency only to invoke the federal-contracting arrangement provided for by statute; where the meaning is simply relevant agency, say so. (13.141; PDF p. 609)
- **R-13.148-complete-and-accurate-contexts** [sev 3] By context: for an obligation to compile data, drop complete, and if possible drop both (Acme shall maintain a database that lists for all customers the following information); to confirm data meet a contract requirement, refer to compliance with the requiring provision (Acme has complied with its obligations under section 5); for copies, drop both, or if nervous keep complete and drop accurate. (13.148, 13.149, 13.151...; PDF p. 610)
- **R-13.163-special-and-consequential** [sev 1] Don't exclude both special damages and consequential damages; U.S. courts generally treat them as synonyms, and special damages means something else in tort, so the pairing adds confusion. (13.163; PDF p. 614)
  - Fix: Use the alternatives in R-13.167-foreseeable-damages and R-13.170-special-circumstances.
- **R-13.164-direct-and-indirect** [sev 1] Don't exclude both direct and indirect damages; that could lead to a fight over whether all damages are excluded or the contract is void. (13.164; PDF p. 614)
  - Fix: State which damages are recoverable.
- **R-13.166-foreseeable-damages** [sev 2] To exclude remote damages, exclude damages that aren't reasonably foreseeable: Neither party will be liable for breach-of-contract damages that the breaching party could not reasonably have foreseen at the time of breach. (13.166, 13.167; PDF p. 615)
- **R-13.168-damages-cap-describe** [sev 2] If the seller wants to exclude otherwise recoverable damages, agree to an absolute cap; if it wants to limit types of damages, describe what damages are included rather than excluded (for a sale of goods, expectancy damages and cover minus contract price, without those terms of art, perhaps with a cap). - **R-13.170-special-circumstances** [sev 2] If you want to exclude damages, don't use consequential damages; use the Hadley second-branch meaning, expressed as special circumstances. (13.170, 13.171, 13.172; PDF p. 615)
- **R-13.173-exclude-other-damages** [sev 2] Exclude other kinds of damages only if doing so serves your interests, not from a copy-and-paste urge, and beware pitfalls such as excluding lost profits. (13.173; PDF p. 617)
- **R-13.178-nonstop** [sev 3] Where you must emphasize nonstop activity, use constant, nonstop, or 24 hours a day, seven days a week instead of continuously. (13.178; PDF p. 618)
- **R-13.180-continuous-offering** [sev 3] Keep continuous in the securities term of art continuous offering (an offering made on a continuous basis). (13.180; PDF p. 618)
- **R-13.190-coterminous-early-termination** [sev 1] When one contract is to terminate when another does, say explicitly whether early termination of one contract terminates the other or whether they operate independently. (13.190; PDF p. 620)
  - Look for: contract whose term is tied to another contract
  - Fix: State whether early termination of either contract carries over to the other.
- **R-13.200-coupled-with-interest-provision** [sev 2] If the agent has an interest in the subject matter of the power and the power should survive death or incompetence, use an explanatory provision instead of the bare formula. (13.200, 13.201; PDF p. 622)
- **R-13.207-amenable-to-cure** [sev 3] Use amenable to cure, not capable of being cured. (13.207; PDF p. 624)
- **R-13.222-default-continuing** [sev 2] In conditions to performance, consider referring to cure instead of has occurred and is continuing; is continuing fits a status (pendency of a lawsuit) but not an event (filing a lawsuit), and an inaccurate statement of fact doesn't continue. - **R-13.234-derogate-from** [sev 2] If you use derogate intransitively (to detract), say derogate from; without from it reads as the transitive 'disparage'. (13.234, 13.236; PDF p. 629)
- **R-13.240-directly-urgency** [sev 3] To express urgency, use promptly, not directly. (13.240; PDF p. 631)
- **R-13.252-duly-terms-of-art** [sev 3] Leave duly alone in terms of art with broader substantive implications, such as duly incorporated and duly organized; there it is shorthand for a parcel of issues. (13.252, 13.253, 13.254; PDF p. 633)
- **R-13.260-continuance** [sev 3] Don't use during continuance of this agreement, and don't use continuance to mean duration; in legal usage continuance usually means postponement of a proceeding. (13.260; PDF p. 635)
- **R-13.270-specifically** [sev 3] Where especially means 'specifically' (a separate committee created especially for this purpose), use specifically. (13.270; PDF p. 637)
- **R-13.280-enter-into** [sev 3] In contexts such as a statement of power and authority, refer to entry into the agreement (enter into this agreement) instead of execute and deliver. (13.280, 13.281, 13.282...; PDF p. 639)
- **R-13.284-sign-and-deliver** [sev 2] Where a party must enter into a contract or sign an ancillary document, or entry is a condition, refer to signature and delivery: have the party sign and deliver a signed copy, the surest way to verify performance or satisfaction of the condition. (13.284; PDF p. 641)
- **R-13.299-no-explanation-required** [sev 2] If the aim is to avoid having to explain a termination, say the company won't have to provide an explanation. (13.299; PDF p. 644)
- **R-13.300-good-faith-acknowledgment** [sev 2] Using for any reason or for no reason doesn't mean the employee has waived the implied duty of good faith; to address that duty, have the employee acknowledge factors the company need not consider. - **R-13.316-avoidance-of-doubt-acknowledge** [sev 3] Where for the avoidance of doubt introduces a fact, have the party acknowledge the fact instead. (13.316; PDF p. 648)
- **R-13.319-for-clarity** [sev 3] The phrase for clarity raises the same issues as for the avoidance of doubt; don't use it. (13.319; PDF p. 649)
- **R-13.337-guarantee-beneficiary** [sev 2] Don't use guarantee as the counterpart of guarantor to mean the person to whom a guarantee is given; given the word's primary meaning, that invites confusion. (13.337; PDF p. 653)
- **R-13.345-guarantees-that-throat-clearing** [sev 2] Don't use guarantees that as throat-clearing (for example, before an obligation or statement of fact that needs no guarantee label); that is a different, disfavored use. (13.345; PDF p. 654)
- **R-13.356-hereby-performance** [sev 3] Keep hereby in language of performance (Acme hereby assigns ...). (13.356; PDF p. 657)
- **R-13.356-there-words-repetition** [sev 3] A there- word is acceptable to avoid awkward or long-winded repetition (... and any amendments thereto); any amendments to it would also work, though most drafters would need to get used to it. (13.356; PDF p. 657)
- **R-13.361-without-limitation** [sev 3] Don't rely on without limitation or but not limited to (or but is not limited to, including without limiting the generality of the foregoing, including without implication of limitation); they are usually redundant and courts have held including restrictive despite them. Use including and includes in a targeted way instead. (13.361, 13.367, 13.369...; PDF p. 658)
- **R-13.376-including-obvious-examples** [sev 2] If you rely on the general word's everyday meaning, don't follow it with a list of obvious examples; fruit, including oranges, lemons, and grapefruit invites a court to limit fruit to citrus. (13.376; PDF p. 662)
- **R-13.378-including-single-example** [sev 3] If you feel compelled to name an obvious member (fruit, including oranges, because oranges matter to the client), a court might have a hard time restricting the class when only one item is named; the more items you add, the greater the risk. (13.378; PDF p. 663)
- **R-13.381-general-word-last** [sev 2] Putting the general word last (oranges, lemons, grapefruit, and other fruit) likely increases the risk of a restrictive reading, because most courts apply ejusdem generis to a list followed by a catchall. Avoid a limited list of obvious members wherever placed; if the client's needs leave no choice, put the general word last and block the limiting implication by adjusting it (and other fruit, whether or not citrus). (13.381, 13.382, 13.383...; PDF p. 664)
- **R-13.392-for-example-list** [sev 2] Don't use for example or e.g. before a list of items after a general word; it raises the same issues as including, and an enlarging item doesn't work after for example. (13.392; PDF p. 666)
- **R-13.393-including-with-limitation** [sev 3] Don't use including with limitation (perhaps a misguided attempt at 'consisting of') or excluding without limitation (what is excluded is limited to what is designated). (13.393, 13.394; PDF p. 666)
- **R-13.395-including-exhaustive** [sev 2] Don't use including to introduce an exhaustive list (Great Britain, including England, Wales, and Scotland); use comprising, or delete the class and list only the components. (13.395; PDF p. 667)
- **R-13.396-including-outside-item** [sev 3] Don't use including to bring in something categorically outside the class (fruit, including carrots; equipment, including software and software license fees); use and (fruit and carrots). (13.396, 13.397, 13.398; PDF p. 667)
- **R-13.399-including-class-modifier** [sev 1] When a modifier applies to the class (red fruit, including apples; any American automobile, including any truck), a court would likely apply the modifier to the items after including, but with a vague modifier (disruptive activity, including playing music) one might argue it doesn't. Be explicit about which meaning you intend. (13.399, 13.400, 13.401...; PDF p. 668)
  - Look for: modifier on the class noun before including
  - Test: Does the class modifier apply to the items after including? If the reader can't tell, the provision is ambiguous.
  - Fix: State whether the modifier applies (for example, add 'that constitute consequential damages', or use and instead of including).
- **R-13.414-import-other-agreement** [sev 3] Incorporation by reference is the right concept for importing, without repeating them, provisions of a separate agreement; even then, describe what is happening rather than use the jargon. (13.414; PDF p. 671)
- **R-13.418-perpetual-release** [sev 3] Don't perpetually (or forever) release someone; you release once. Avoid forever and until (or to) the end of time or of the world. (13.418, 13.419; PDF p. 672)
- **R-13.435-indemnify-party-claims** [sev 1] If you want indemnification to cover claims between the parties (not just nonparty claims), say so in the indemnification provisions, or risk a fight. (13.435, 13.436, 13.437; PDF p. 675)
  - Look for: indemnification provision silent on direct claims between the parties
  - Test: Does the provision say whether it covers claims by one party against the other?
  - Fix: State expressly that indemnification covers claims between the parties (or only nonparty claims).
- **R-13.440-hold-harmless** [sev 1] Use just indemnify, not indemnify and hold harmless (or save harmless); never use hold harmless. Some courts and commentators give hold harmless a different meaning, so the redundancy is risky. (13.440, 13.441, 13.446...; PDF p. 676)
  - Fix: indemnify
- **R-13.451-keep-indemnified** [sev 3] Don't use shall indemnify and keep indemnified; it serves no discernible purpose. (13.451; PDF p. 679)
- **R-13.452-defend** [sev 2] Don't rely on adding defend; address explicitly, in the indemnification procedure provisions, how defense of nonparty claims is handled. (13.452; PDF p. 679)
- **R-13.453-indemnify-prepositions** [sev 3] Use a single preposition after indemnify (for, from, or against, depending on what follows); don't use the rhetorical triplet for, from, and against. Courts have relied on prepositions to hold that indemnification doesn't cover claims between the parties, but that happens only when the drafter doesn't express the key issues, so address them. (13.453, 13.454, 13.455...; PDF p. 680)
- **R-13.474-inure-trademark** [sev 3] In trademark licenses, instead of saying goodwill inures solely to the benefit of the licensor, address the goodwill issue directly. (13.474; PDF p. 684)
- **R-13.479-understood-acknowledges** [sev 3] Where it being understood that means a party accepts as accurate a fact asserted by another party, use acknowledges. (13.479; PDF p. 686)
- **R-13.492-several-not-joint** [sev 1] Don't use several but not joint; where it is meant to apportion liability among owners pro rata, state the pro rata recovery and the right to bring separate actions. (13.492, 13.496; PDF p. 689)
  - Fix: Acme may recover from each Shareholder a proportion of any collective liability of the Shareholders under this agreement equal to the proportion of all Shares then outstanding represented by the Shares then owned by that Shareholder. Acme may bring 1 or more separate actions ...
- **R-13.497-joint-several-statutes** [sev 2] Statutes in many jurisdictions make joint promises joint and several or presume joint and several duties; expressing the several concept explicitly won't hurt even if a statute would supply it, and it tells the parties what the law provides. (13.497, 13.498; PDF p. 690)
- **R-13.504-best-of-knowledge** [sev 2] Don't refer to the best of someone's knowledge; best is a rhetorical flourish and could suggest a duty to investigate or heightened knowledge. (13.504; PDF p. 692)
- **R-13.505-knowledge-and-belief** [sev 2] Don't use knowledge and belief; it is redundant and invites a disgruntled party to invest it with meaning. (13.505; PDF p. 692)
- **R-13.512-is-unaware-of** [sev 2] Use knowledge, not is unaware of; it would be reckless to assume a contract can create a meaningful distinction between absence of knowledge and knowledge of absence, and knowledge is more versatile. (13.512, 13.513; PDF p. 695)
- **R-13.515-latinisms-ingrained** [sev 3] Accept Latinisms so ingrained they are English, such as pro rata, status quo, and vice versa. (13.515; PDF p. 696)
- **R-13.516-attorneys-apostrophe** [sev 2] If you use attorneys' fees, put the apostrophe after the s; attorney's fees invites a fight over whether it means one attorney's fees. (13.516; PDF p. 696)
- **R-13.526-reasonably-likely** [sev 2] Don't say reasonably likely; it adds vagueness to a degree of probability, and reasonableness has nothing to do with likelihood. (13.526; PDF p. 698)
- **R-13.537-lump-sum-specialized** [sev 3] Keep lump sum where it has a specialized meaning, as in construction and some other fields (for example, a lump-sum contract price). (13.537; PDF p. 700)
- **R-13.571-termination-for-crime** [sev 2] In a termination-for-crime provision, keep it simple unless you have a good reason to complicate it. A reasonable pro-employer formulation: the Employee is charged with a crime that (1) is punishable by a custodial penalty, instead of or in addition to a fine or other noncustodial penalty, or (2) is related to the Employee's employment. An employment agreement might add a broader right to terminate for conduct the employee knew, or a reasonable person would have known, would reasonably be expected to have a material adverse effect on the company's business or reputation. (13.571, 13.572, 13.573; PDF p. 708)
- **R-13.579-mutual-agreement** [sev 3] Don't use mutual with agreement, or mutually with agree or agreeable; reciprocity is inherent in agreement. (13.579, 13.583; PDF p. 710)
- **R-13.584-mutually-jointly** [sev 3] Don't use mutually when you mean jointly. (13.584; PDF p. 711)
- **R-13.602-intent-consequences** [sev 1] Use intentional or intentionally instead of willful or willfully, and make the labels relate to the consequences of the party's action, not the action itself; one can act intentionally without intending damage. (13.602, 13.603; PDF p. 715)
  - Fix: intentionally causing those consequences
- **R-13.604-tort-standards-governing-law** [sev 2] Adjust tort-based standards to the governing law; if it doesn't recognize U.S. concepts, don't insist on them. Don't define recklessness or any form of it; a definition adds verbiage without certainty. (13.604, 13.605; PDF p. 715)
- **R-13.606-tort-carve-out-cap** [sev 2] Consider not using tort-based standards for performance under the contract; the contract already gives a remedy for nonperformance. In particular, a recklessness or intentional-misconduct carve-out from an indemnification cap could undermine the cap if indemnification covers failure to comply with obligations; such a carve-out makes more sense for losses from relations with nonparties. (13.606; PDF p. 715)
- **R-13.607-processor-sample** [sev 2] Model provision replacing willful misconduct or gross negligence: liability only to the extent that, as a result of reckless disregard for the consequences of an act or failure to act, or of intentionally causing those consequences, the party causes damages. (13.607; PDF p. 716)
- **R-13.611-not-less-than** [sev 3] Use not less than, not no less than; no less connotes surprise, while not less is matter-of-fact. (13.611, 13.612; PDF p. 717)
- **R-13.635-notwithstanding-the-foregoing** [sev 1] Notwithstanding the foregoing is ambiguous: the foregoing might refer to the previous sentence, all preceding text, or something in between. (13.635; PDF p. 722)
  - HOUSE: partly superseded: House rule 4: where a reference is needed, name the provision by its heading, not its number (the section titled "Payment"); better, restructure so no reference is needed.
  - Test: Can the reader tell exactly which text the foregoing refers to?
  - Fix: Name the provision (subject to section 3.2).
- **R-13.638-subject-to** [sev 2] Qualify the provision being undercut with subject to section X rather than prefacing the undercutting provision with notwithstanding; don't use both; and generally be specific about which provision does the undercutting. (13.638, 13.641; PDF p. 723)
- **R-13.639-notwithstanding-other-side** [sev 3] Notwithstanding has a use even for drafters who normally use subject to: when proposing a change to the other side's draft that undercuts other provisions, especially a last-minute change, it lets you make one change rather than add the provision plus cross-references. (13.639; PDF p. 724)
- **R-13.640-subject-to-terms** [sev 3] Subject to the terms of this agreement before key language of performance or obligation can be superfluous, since rights and obligations come from the contract as a whole; but when a key obligation (such as to cause a merger) is subject to conditions and termination rights, it is generally simpler to say Subject to the terms of this agreement than to list the sections. (13.640, 13.641; PDF p. 724)
- **R-13.642-except-as-provided-in** [sev 2] Know the difference: except as provided in marks the other provision as an exception, so it is narrower than subject to, which could mean an exception or a supplement (such as a condition on a right). By extension, except as otherwise provided in this agreement is narrower than subject to the terms of this agreement. (13.642; PDF p. 725)
- **R-13.643-express-interplay** [sev 1] Besides expressing which provision prevails, express clearly the nature of the interplay between the provisions. (13.643; PDF p. 725)
  - Look for: two provisions that address the same subject differently
  - Fix: Say what the overriding provision does to the other (excepts, conditions, limits).
- **R-13.644-nullified-provisions** [sev 1] Don't leave in the contract language nullified by new text (signaled with subject to, notwithstanding, or provisos); a party can claim the ostensibly nullified language still has life. Delete it. (13.644, 13.645; PDF p. 725)
  - Look for: provision overridden in full by a later provision but left in the text
  - Fix: Delete the nullified language.
  - Exceptions: Keeping the other side's language may help get the deal done (13.645), but the risk remains.
- **R-13.648-novation-combined** [sev 3] If keeping the word novation matters, combine it with the explanation: does not effect a novation of the Obligations, in that it will not result in any of the Obligations being replaced. (13.648; PDF p. 726)
- **R-13.657-one-hand-two-items** [sev 3] Omit on the one hand ... on the other hand with a list of only two items, or with a list of three or more items that doesn't need bifurcating. (13.657, 13.660; PDF p. 728)
- **R-13.658-considered-collectively** [sev 2] Where one of two items is plural and the phrase is used to show that its members act together, say so with (considered collectively), placing the plural item first to avoid syntactic ambiguity. (13.658, 13.659; PDF p. 729)
- **R-13.665-parentheses-within-words** [sev 3] Don't use parentheses within a word, such as (s) on a singular noun (say one or more), (sub)licensee, (sub)contractor, (self)insured, or (s)he. (13.665, 13.666; PDF p. 731)
- **R-13.689-prevailing-party-california** [sev 2] For contracts governed by California law, the Cal. Civil Code s. 1717(b)(1) definition of prevailing party for contract claims is mandatory; a contract definition can't alter or avoid it. (13.689; PDF p. 736)
- **R-13.709-public-domain-ip** [sev 3] If you use in the public domain in its intellectual-property sense (works no longer subject to copyright), consider explaining it; simpler still, omit it and say what you mean. (13.709, 13.710, 13.711; PDF p. 741)
- **R-13.715-reasonably-redundant** [sev 3] Omit reasonably where it is redundant: with verbs that inherently involve reasonableness (cooperate reasonably), with promptly and other such adverbs (reasonably promptly), and in reasonably likely. (13.715, 13.716, 13.717; PDF p. 742)
- **R-13.724-good-faith-belief** [sev 3] Don't use good faith with words that refer to a party's actual state of mind; good-faith belief makes no sense. (13.724; PDF p. 744)
- **R-13.725-good-faith-default** [sev 3] In jurisdictions recognizing the implied duty of good faith, stating a good-faith standard isn't necessary, though a reminder can help. The difference between the standards can be more apparent than real, since courts often judge good faith by how others behaved, which may help if the other side balks at reasonableness. (13.725, 13.726; PDF p. 744)
- **R-13.727-both-standards** [sev 3] Usually don't use both standards in one provision; reasonable good-faith efforts and reasonable good-faith judgment are redundant. You might want both where conduct must be reasonable and not a pretext (taxes contested in good faith by appropriate proceedings), but both can be overkill (a customer disputing charges reasonably and in good faith invites an argument of bad faith). (13.727, 13.728, 13.729; PDF p. 745)
- **R-13.746-respectively** [sev 3] Use respectively only to pair each item in an earlier list with its counterpart in a following list with the same number of items, both joined by and, not or; it is redundant without a preceding list to echo. (13.746, 13.747, 13.748; PDF p. 749)
- **R-13.784-sole-and-exclusive** [sev 3] Don't use sole and exclusive: say sole remedy or only remedy instead of sole and exclusive remedy; exclusive jurisdiction in a forum-selection provision; and Acme owns all those interests instead of the sole and exclusive property of Acme. At its sole and exclusive option raises the same issues as at its sole discretion. (13.784, 13.785, 13.786; PDF p. 758)
- **R-13.798-subrogation-insurance** [sev 3] In insurance-requirement provisions, changing subrogation terminology means presenting insurers with novel wording; consult an insurance specialist or leave well enough alone. (13.798; PDF p. 761)
- **R-13.801-subsisting-ip** [sev 3] Don't parrot the copyright statute's subsists (Copyright Act s. 102) in statements about IP; use the word that makes sense more broadly, such as exists. (13.801, 13.802, 13.803...; PDF p. 762)
- **R-13.805-subsistence-certificate** [sev 3] Subsisting is sporadically used instead of existing; where a state issues a certificate of subsistence rather than a certificate of good standing (Pennsylvania is one), it is easy to see why a drafter would use subsisting for an entity formed there. (13.805; PDF p. 763)
- **R-13.821-suffer-transitive** [sev 3] Suffer meaning 'to allow, especially by reason of indifference' (suffer to exist) is pompous and archaic; usually use permit. (13.821; PDF p. 767)
- **R-13.823-survival-claims** [sev 3] Don't state that claims arising before termination survive termination; terminating, even for breach, doesn't preclude a damages claim. (13.823, 13.824, 13.825; PDF p. 767)
- **R-13.826-survival-provisions** [sev 3] To make an obligation continue after termination, build its duration into the obligation (during the term of this agreement and for five years thereafter) rather than stating that the provision survives; don't state that dispute-resolution and other boilerplate survive termination. (13.826, 13.827, 13.828; PDF p. 768)
- **R-13.829-survival-statements** [sev 2] Instead of stating how long statements of fact survive, address time limits on indemnification claims directly and more broadly in a section entitled Time Limits; survival periods address only one basis of claims and don't limit claims for breach of obligations. (13.829, 13.830, 13.831; PDF p. 769)
- **R-13.839-terminate-and-expire** [sev 3] Don't use terminate and expire together; it shows inconsistency or redundancy. (13.839; PDF p. 771)
- **R-13.856-without-cause-symmetry** [sev 3] If the contract also provides for termination for cause, you could use termination without cause instead of for any reason, for symmetry. Eliminate or for no reason. For a heading, if Termination for Any Reason seems blunt, use Unrestricted Termination or group by who may terminate (Buyer Termination). (13.856, 13.857, 13.858...; PDF p. 775)
- **R-13.868-whichever-is** [sev 3] If you use whichever is, use or ((1) ... or (2) ..., whichever is later), and reserve it for items described succinctly, because it puts the basis of selection at the end. (13.868, 13.869; PDF p. 778)
- **R-13.881-third-party-beneficiary** [sev 3] You can't dispense entirely with third party: keep it in terms of art such as third-party beneficiary and where legislation uses it. (13.881; PDF p. 780)
- **R-13.901-extent-permitted-performance** [sev 3] In language of performance such as a jury-trial waiver, to the extent permitted by law does nothing except tell the parties the waiver might or might not be enforceable; if you want to signal willingness to have an unenforceable provision modified, use a severability provision instead. (13.901, 13.902; PDF p. 784)

### Chapter 15: Internal Rules of Interpretation

- **R-15.6-interpretation-unless-context** [sev 2] Don't open an internal rule of interpretation with Unless the context otherwise requires; it undercuts the certainty the provision is meant to offer. (15.6; PDF p. 821)

### Chapter 17: Drafting as Writing

- **R-17.19-wordy-phrases** [sev 3] Replace wordy phrases with simpler ones: prior to -> before; subsequent to -> after; in lieu of -> instead of; in the event that / in the event of -> if; pursuant to -> under, in accordance with, authorized by; set forth in -> stated in, in; until such time as -> until; in order to -> to; for the purpose of [verb]ing -> to [verb]; by reason of -> because of; due to the fact that -> because; despite the fact that -> although; is unable to -> cannot; is binding upon -> binds; per annum -> a year; with the exception of -> except for; and the other entries in the 17.19 table. (17.19, 17.20; PDF p. 851)
- **R-17.22-the-fact-that** [sev 3] The fact that can almost always be improved (by virtue of the fact that -> because; The fact that there may be no Loans outstanding -> Whether any Loans are outstanding); notwithstanding the fact that and regardless of the fact that can always be replaced by although, even though, or even if. (17.22, 17.23; PDF p. 852)
- **R-17.25-with-respect-to** [sev 3] Replace with respect to with regarding or a single preposition (in, for, on), a shorter phrase (as to), or a bigger fix (a Fund's Borrowing Instructions). (17.25, 17.26, 17.27; PDF p. 854)

### Chapter 18: Amendments

- **R-18.5-variation** [sev 3] Don't use variation or vary for amending a contract; use amendment and amend. Synonyms cause waste and mischief, and in standard English a variation is something new, not a modified original. (18.5, 18.6, 18.7; PDF p. 859)
- **R-18.13-amend-vs-supplement** [sev 3] An amendment changes the original; a supplement adds to it. A second agreement amends only if it says its provisions are added to the original; if its provisions are freestanding, call it a supplement, not the fusty Latinism addendum. (18.13; PDF p. 861)

### Chapter 19: Letter Agreements

- **R-19.7-letter-salutation** [sev 3] For an individual recipient, use Dear plus the name, reflecting the relationship; for an entity, dispense with a salutation. Don't use Dear Sirs (gender-specific, old-fashioned), Dear Sirs and Madams, Ladies and Gentlemen, or To whom it may concern. (19.7, 19.8; PDF p. 866)
