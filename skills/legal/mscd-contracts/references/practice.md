# Drafting and review practice

- **Annotation first and the other side's drafts:** see SKILL.md, "2 principles" (the AI post, mid-2023), and `house-style.md` section 10.
- **Caselaw.** MSCD never relies on caselaw to fix a usage's meaning; cases show which usages risk disputes (Introduction, PDF p. 42).

> **Book examples are in MSCD5's style, not house style.** They may use lowercase *schedule*, *1 January 2024*, spelled-out numbers, 1.1 numbering, *the* before party names, or a passive that MSCD accepts. Restate any example in house style (`house-style.md`) before you put it in a contract or a comment. For the other side's drafts, the house rule is to flag everything, whatever a rule's "let-pass" or "style-only" label says.

### Introduction

- **R-intro-ai-review** [sev 3] Treat AI contract review with caution: if its patterns come from traditional contracts, expect 'garbage in, garbage out.' AI review is more promising when combined with expertise about what to look for and what to tell the user. (Introduction; PDF p. 60)
- **R-intro-caselaw-not-meaning** [sev 3] Don't rely on caselaw to give a usage a specific meaning. Draft so the contract speaks directly to the reader in standard English. An errant court opinion is no reason to retreat from a recommendation. (Introduction; PDF p. 41)
- **R-intro-drafting-cover-note** [sev 3] Consider explaining your usages to the other side in advance, in a cover note that cites the manual and asks them not to add traditional usages unless that would make the contract clearer or better reflect the deal. (Introduction; PDF p. 53)
- **R-intro-drafting-deal-context** [sev 3] Match how far you depart from traditional usage to the transaction: in a one-off, bet-the-company deal expect resistance to novel language; in a high-volume template, strip out anything wordy or extraneous and resist changes that restore dysfunction. (Introduction; PDF p. 53)
- **R-intro-drafting-expect-objections** [sev 3] When drafting, expect and prepare for objections to unfamiliar prose, chiefly from the other side but also from more senior people on your side. (Introduction; PDF p. 52)
- **R-intro-drafting-incremental** [sev 3] Be realistic: with traditional templates or industry forms you may have little room. Even with control, retool incrementally, starting with the provisions you use most. (Introduction; PDF p. 54)
- **R-intro-drafting-lowercase-agreement** [sev 3] Consider holding firm even on lowercase this agreement. It has no deal significance, and someone who insists on a capital A signals early that they will meddle without good reason. (Introduction; PDF p. 54)
- **R-intro-drafting-senior-people** [sev 3] With a traditionalist senior colleague who isn't receptive, give them what they expect on usages that don't affect meaning, but be 'gently persistent' on issues that might affect the deal or create confusion. (Introduction; PDF p. 53)
- **R-intro-examples-not-models** [sev 3] Except as the book indicates, treat its examples as illustrations, not models. Use as model language only text the book recommends (for example, a table entry rated ✓✓ or an 'after' version). (Introduction; PDF p. 40)
- **R-intro-excuse-means-what-people-think** [sev 3] Reject the claim that a confusing usage means whatever the drafter or negotiator thinks it means. In a dispute the question is what meaning the parties attributed to the usage. (Introduction; PDF p. 49)
- **R-intro-excuse-risky-to-change** [sev 3] Weigh the fear that replacing a traditional usage causes mischief: a counterparty might push back and slow the deal, but litigators are unlikely to give a clearer usage an unexpected meaning ('It's difficult to make mischief with clear'). (Introduction; PDF p. 50)
- **R-intro-excuse-shared-understanding** [sev 3] Don't rely on a claimed shared understanding of what a confusing usage means. The claim rests on sweeping generalization and argumentum ad populum, and it doesn't help if the usage is clearly ambiguous. (Introduction; PDF p. 48)
- **R-intro-excuse-tested** [sev 3] Don't keep a usage because courts have 'tested' it. Courts have scrutinized only some terminology; they don't all give it the same meaning; and if a court had to determine meaning, the wording failed to state intent clearly. (Introduction; PDF p. 47)
- **R-intro-excuse-works** [sev 3] Don't accept the argument that traditional contract language 'works.' Contracts are more or less clear and present more or less risk of dispute; caselaw is full of disputes caused by traditional usages. (Introduction; PDF p. 47)
- **R-intro-org-clarity-criterion** [sev 3] Use clarity as the only criterion for choosing a usage. Where alternatives exist, use the clearest; where they are equally clear, have everyone in the organization use the same one. (Introduction; PDF p. 57)
- **R-intro-org-nonlawyers** [sev 3] Let competent nonlawyers (businesspeople, contract managers, paralegals) take a greater role in expressing deal terms, provided they have a good command of contract language and substance. (Introduction; PDF p. 58)
- **R-intro-org-style-guide** [sev 3] To make an organization's contracts clear and consistent, first adopt a style guide; the manual can serve as its foundation. Then train personnel and provide clear, modern templates. (Introduction; PDF p. 55)
- **R-intro-review-cite-manual** [sev 3] If the other drafter is a traditionalist, cite the manual to explain a requested change, to show it rests on an internationally recognized set of guidelines. (Introduction; PDF p. 54)
- **R-intro-review-flag** [sev 1] On the other side's draft, flag anything that should be a condition but is expressed as an obligation, inherently confusing usages (for example, indemnify and hold harmless), and every instance of ambiguity. (Introduction; PDF p. 54)
- **R-intro-review-flag-dispute-glitches** [sev 1] Even seemingly minor glitches are worth flagging on the other side's draft if they have caused disputes, for example throat-clearing verb structures and may in restrictive relative clauses. (Introduction; PDF p. 54)
- **R-intro-review-let-pass** [sev 3] On the other side's draft, give a pass to traditional features that don't make sense but aren't confusing: a traditional recital of consideration, benign overuse of shall, and archaisms in the front and back of the contract. (Introduction; PDF p. 54)
- **R-intro-review-scope** [sev 1] When reviewing the other side's draft, look only for what doesn't reflect the deal as you understand it and what could create confusion. Don't try to make their draft 'a thing of beauty.' (Introduction; PDF p. 54)
- **R-intro-scope-business-contracts** [sev 3] Apply this manual to business contracts: contracts between businesses experienced in handling transactions or represented by lawyers. It doesn't address consumer contracts, although much of it could apply to them. (Introduction; PDF p. 39)

### Chapter 1: Characteristics of Optimal Contract Language (opening)

- **R-1.1-limited-function** [sev 3] Draft with the limited function of business contracts in mind: they only regulate conduct, state facts, and allocate risk. Don't treat contracts as just another form of legal writing. (1.1; PDF p. 61)
- **R-1.3-apply-principles** [sev 3] Apply the 12 principles of optimal contract language when assessing any usage the manual doesn't examine. (1.2, 1.3; PDF p. 61)

### Chapter 1: Don't Expect Contract Language to Be Simple

- **R-1.74-not-simple** [sev 3] Don't try to make a contract simpler than the transaction it expresses; contracts are necessarily as complex as their transactions. (1.72, 1.73, 1.74; PDF p. 77)

### Chapter 13: Selected Usages

- **R-13.53-dispute-strategy** [sev 2] Recognize that deciding which claims a provision covers can backfire; if you do many transactions, apply a flexible, reasoned (not copy-and-pasted) dispute-resolution strategy and focus, deal by deal, on eliminating sources of dispute and confusion. (13.53, 13.54; PDF p. 589)
- **R-13.562-crime-provision-elements** [sev 2] When drafting a termination-for-crime provision, decide: what crime (crime is preferable to criminal offense; felony and misdemeanor aren't recognized everywhere and vary by state); involving what offense (labels such as dishonesty or fraud, or a crime related to the employee's employment); other criteria (carve-outs such as noncustodial penalties or DWI); at what stage (commission, arrest, indictment, charge, or conviction or plea); which jurisdiction (name one if terminology varies); and whether to combine kinds of crime or stages. (13.562, 13.563, 13.564...; PDF p. 705)
